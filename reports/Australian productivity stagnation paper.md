# Australia Swapped Productivity Growth for Hours

Australia's output per hour has barely grown since 2019 because both of its engines stopped working at the same time. Multifactor productivity (MFP) has been flat to negative, and capital per hour has fallen. On the paper's own OECD Productivity Database calculations (whole economy, calendar years), **GDP per hour grew 0.10% a year over 2019–25, against 1.88% in the United States, 0.44% in Canada and 0.53% in the EU27**. Australia's level fell from **95% of the US in 2000 to 81% in 2025** ([OECD Productivity Database](https://sdmx.oecd.org/public/rest/data/OECD.SDD.TPS,DSD_PDB@DF_PDB,2.0/)). Over 2020–24, the OECD puts Australia's MFP contribution at **−0.12 pp against +1.34 pp for the US**, and its capital-deepening contribution at **−0.11 pp against +0.54 pp**. By that arithmetic, about two-thirds of the Australia–US gap is MFP and one-third is capital (our derivation). Australian living standards still rose by about 1% a year per head, but only because **hours worked per head rose 5.3%**. That is a job-rich, productivity-poor recovery, and it resembles France and Italy far more than the US. Australia is not an outlier against Canada, the UK or continental Europe. All of them had weak MFP and weak capital deepening. The US, with a post-2019 TFP acceleration, a capital-intensity boom and a surge in business formation, is the exception. Four features are distinctive to Australia: a policy-driven expansion of an input-measured care economy that mechanically drags on measured productivity (about 0.3 pp a year); long declines in measured MFP in mining and electricity (about −34% and −35% cumulative since 2001–02) that reflect depletion, weather and the build phase of the energy transition; a non-mining investment share that has never recovered from the post-GFC slump; and two decades of fading competition and business dynamism. Fixing the measurement shrinks the problem but does not make it go away. The binding policy constraint is implementing reforms that have already been diagnosed, not diagnosing them again.

## Australia's stagnation is unmistakable, but its size depends on which statistic you read

### The headline: zero hourly growth while the US pulled away

The paper's OECD calculations show a level of GDP per hour that is almost unchanged: **US$68.6 per hour in 2019 and US$69.0 in 2025** (constant 2020 PPP). Over the same period US output per hour rose from US$76.6 to US$85.6 ([OECD Productivity Database](https://sdmx.oecd.org/public/rest/data/OECD.SDD.TPS,DSD_PDB@DF_PDB,2.0/)). The slowdown did not start in 2019. Australian hourly productivity growth fell from **1.33% a year in 2005–14 to 0.74% in 2014–19, and then to 0.10%**. The RBA describes labour productivity as "currently sitting at around 2019 levels" and cut its trend productivity assumption from **1.0% to 0.7%** in August 2025 ([RBA 2026 Senate submission](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/submissions/labour-market-and-inflation/productivity-in-australia-02-2026/)). The latest data have not turned. Labour productivity **fell 0.2% over the year to June 2026**, because output growth of 2.1% was outpaced by hours growth of about 2.3–2.4% ([PC Productivity update, September 2026](https://www.pc.gov.au/ongoing/productivity-insights/update-september-2026/)).

The table makes one point plainly. Australia, France and Italy raised hours per head strongly and had little or no hourly productivity growth. The US, Germany, the UK and Canada had flat or falling hours per head. Australia's 0.97% annual growth in GDP per capita is almost exactly its 0.87% annual growth in hours per head (our derivation from the 5.3% six-year rise) plus its 0.10% hourly productivity growth. **Effectively all of Australia's per-capita gain since 2019 came from working more hours per person.** That source of growth cannot be repeated indefinitely.

### Why the OECD whole-economy and ABS/PC market-sector figures disagree

The official Australian numbers that most commentators quote look less dire than the OECD series, and an academic paper has to reconcile them explicitly. The ABS market-sector series, on an hours-worked basis, gives annual labour productivity and MFP growth of **1.6/−0.1 (2019–20), 1.6/0.7 (2020–21), 2.3/2.4 (2021–22), −3.6/−0.9 (2022–23), 1.0/0.1 (2023–24) and −0.2/−0.5 (2024–25)** ([ABS, Estimates of Industry MFP 2024–25](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/industry/industry-overview/estimates-industry-multifactor-productivity/latest-release)). Averaging those log changes gives market-sector labour productivity of about **0.45% a year over the six years from 2019–20, but only 0.22% over the five years from 2020–21**. MFP averages about 0.28% and 0.36% respectively (our derivation). The implied capital-deepening contribution changes sign with the start year: **about +0.17 pp a year if 2019–20 is included and about −0.14 pp if it is excluded**. The second figure is close to the OECD's −0.11 pp.

There are six sources of difference, and each one points the same way: the OECD figure is lower.

**Coverage.** The ABS publishes MFP only for the market sector. The OECD figure covers the whole economy, including health, education and public administration. Output in those industries is largely measured by inputs, so measured productivity there is pinned near zero, and it has recently been falling ([ABS, Understanding productivity statistics](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/detailed-methodology-information/information-papers/understanding-abs-productivity-statistics)). The PC's quarterly series shows how large the coverage effect is. Since June 2014 market-sector productivity has risen **7.5%**, non-market productivity has fallen **2.5%**, and the whole economy has risen only **3.6%** ([PC Sept 2026](https://www.pc.gov.au/ongoing/productivity-insights/update-september-2026/)).

**Output concept.** The OECD measures GDP, while the ABS market sector measures gross value added.

**Timing.** Financial-year windows that start in 2019–20 capture the COVID composition spike. Over the year to June 2020, low-productivity hospitality and retail hours fell out of the denominator. A calendar-2019 base sits just before that spike.

**Start-year sensitivity.** In the PC quarterly series, whole-economy growth is about 0.15% a year from June 2019 but about 0.02% a year from December 2019. Non-market growth ranges from −0.34% to −0.92% a year depending on which of these two quarters is the base (our derivation).

**Labour-input basis.** On a quality-adjusted labour input basis, ABS market-sector MFP in 2024–25 was −0.8%, against −0.5% on hours ([ABS 2026](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/industry/industry-overview/estimates-industry-multifactor-productivity/latest-release)).

**Arithmetic and vintage.** The PC uses percentage changes and the ABS uses log changes. That is why the PC reports mining MFP at −3.2% and agriculture at +10.4% for 2024–25, while the ABS reports −3.3% and +9.9% ([PC Annual productivity bulletin 2026](https://assets.pc.gov.au/2026-02/productivity-bulletin-2026.pdf)). The 2024–25 ABS release also incorporates the 2025 annual national accounts revisions and a methodological revision of Labour Account hours. Any 2019–25 hours profile therefore depends on the vintage, and the paper should cite it as "ABS 5260.0.55.002, February 2026 release".

The ILO's modelled series puts Australia at **0.84% a year**, well above the OECD's 0.10%. Even so, it still shows a gap of about 1.5 pp a year to the US, whose ILO figure is **2.30%**. The cross-country *gap* is therefore robust across sources even though the Australian *level* of growth is not. The ILO series should be used only for within-source comparisons, such as with Southeast Asia.

Level comparisons add a further layer. On OECD constant-PPP data, the US was about 24% above Australia in 2025. The PC, using OECD "2020 onwards" PPPs, finds US *non-agricultural market-sector* labour productivity **23% above Australia's** in 2023 ([PC 2026 bulletin](https://assets.pc.gov.au/2026-02/productivity-bulletin-2026.pdf)). e61 puts Australian *private-sector* productivity about **12% below the US**, with almost all of the gap opening since 2016 ([e61 policy report 2025](https://e61.in/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/Australia_s_key_differences_policy_report.pdf)). The ICP 2021 PPP revisions moved Australia, Canada and the UK "further from top OECD performers" ([OECD ICP 2021 release](https://www.oecd.org/en/data/insights/statistical-releases/2024/05/share-of-oecd-economies-in-global-gdp-broadly-stable-at-46-in-2021-compared-to-2017.html)). Before those revisions, Treasury had put Australia mostly at 75–85% of US GDP per hour over 40 years ([Treasury, "Comparing productivity"](https://www.treasury.gov.au/sites/default/files/2019-03/04_comparing_productivity.pdf)). The decline from 95% to 81% should be described as a decline *in the current OECD vintage*. A DBnomics mirror of the OECD levels dataset shows an apparent break in the Australian current-PPP series, from about 78.4 in 2022 to about 160.6 in 2023, so the levels file needs checking before any figure is published ([DBnomics OECD PDB_LV](https://db.nomics.world/OECD/DSD_PDB@DF_PDB_LV/AUS.A.GDPHRS._T.USD_PPP_H.V._Z._Z._Z)).

## Neither MFP nor capital deepening is carrying Australian growth

### Australia's MFP has been weak for twenty years, not five

The ABS growth-cycle data put the post-2019 slump in context. Market-sector MFP averaged **1.0% a year over 1998–99 to 2003–04, −0.2% over 2003–04 to 2009–10 (the mining-boom investment phase plus drought), 0.7% over 2009–10 to 2017–18, and 0.8% over the incomplete 2017–18 to 2021–22 cycle** ([ABS 2026](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/industry/industry-overview/estimates-industry-multifactor-productivity/latest-release)). The PC notes that MFP's 20-year average of **0.4% a year** compares with **1.6% a year over 1994–95 to 2003–04** ([PC 2026 bulletin](https://assets.pc.gov.au/2026-02/productivity-bulletin-2026.pdf)). MFP fell in **9 of 16 industries** in 2024–25 ([ABS 2026](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/industry/industry-overview/estimates-industry-multifactor-productivity/latest-release)), so the weakness is broad. The PC's 2025 feature describes Australia since the mid-2000s as "learning but not always doing": it has found fewer new ways of combining inputs and made poor use of existing ideas ([Ashburner & Wong, PC 2025 bulletin](https://www.pc.gov.au/ongoing/productivity-insights/bulletins/bulletin-2025/)).

Composition flatters even these weak numbers. The RBA's real-time labour-quality index finds that rising education and experience accounted for **about two-thirds of market-sector labour productivity growth from 2018/19 to 2023/24**. On its quality-adjusted measure, growth was about 0.3% a year, against about 0.75% in the ABS headline ([Bruno, Hambur & Wang 2025](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/bulletin/2025/jul/a-closer-to-real-time-labour-quality-index.html)). The implication for an academic paper is that **quality-adjusted MFP plus capital deepening was close to zero over the period**. The post-2019 problem is worse than hours-based figures suggest, not better. One further caveat runs the other way. The ABS QALI index interpolates linearly between the 2016 and 2021 Censuses and extrapolates after 2021. The RBA's LLFS-based index shows labour quality rising less during the pandemic than QALI implies, so composition adjustments are themselves uncertain.

### Capital deepening stalled, and the 2023–24 rebound was its last gasp

The PC attributed the 1.1% rise in market-sector labour productivity in 2023–24 "almost entirely" to capital deepening, with MFP up only 0.1% ([PC 2025 bulletin](https://www.pc.gov.au/ongoing/productivity-insights/bulletins/bulletin-2025/)). In 2024–25 labour productivity fell 0.2% and MFP fell 0.5%, which implies a residual capital-deepening contribution of about +0.3 pp (our derivation). Capital services grew 1.8% and hours 1.0% ([ABS 2026](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/industry/industry-overview/estimates-industry-multifactor-productivity/latest-release)). Over longer windows the picture is stagnation. The RBA reports capital per worker "broadly unchanged for around five years before the pandemic" and "currently a bit below those pre-pandemic levels" ([Plumb 2025](https://www.rba.gov.au/speeches/2025/sp-so-2025-02-27.html)). The PC says Australia has had "little capital deepening over the past decade" because it has invested less of national income in new capital ([PC Quarterly bulletin, Sept 2025](https://www.pc.gov.au/ongoing/productivity-insights/bulletins/quarterly-bulletin-september-2025/)).

Christensen's 2026 PC feature adds a capital-*productivity* angle. Market-sector capital productivity fell **18.6% between 1995 and 2025**, mostly during the mining boom. At comparable capital intensity, the Netherlands and the US are about **12% more productive** than Australia, using Cobb-Douglas with a capital share of 0.48. Market-sector R&D as a share of gross fixed capital formation fell by **more than 40%** over the same 30 years ([Christensen, PC 2026 bulletin](https://assets.pc.gov.au/2026-02/productivity-bulletin-2026.pdf)). Taken together, this suggests that roughly half of the US level advantage reflects more capital per hour and half reflects using that capital better.

### The US is the outlier; Canada, the UK and Europe look like Australia

The comparison changes how the problem should be framed. BLS private nonfarm business TFP grew **1.0% a year over 2019–25, against 0.6% over 2007–19**. Capital intensity grew 2.3–2.7% a year in 2024–25, and labour composition contributed 0.3–0.4 pp ([BLS TFP 2025 release](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/prod3_03192026.pdf)). The OECD's +1.34 pp US MFP contribution for 2020–24 is larger than the BLS TFP figure. Three things explain the difference: whole-economy versus private-business coverage, a window that excludes 2019 and 2025, and the fact that BLS TFP nets out labour composition while the OECD MFP measure does not. Footnote this whenever the two are cited together.

The other comparators look much more like Australia. Canadian business MFP averaged about **−0.1% a year over 2019–22** ([StatCan Daily, April 2024](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/daily-quotidien/240416/dq240416b-eng.htm)) and fell **1.7% in 2023**, with capital intensity a drag for a third straight year ([Nova Scotia Finance](https://novascotia.ca/finance/statistics/news.asp?id=20899)). UK market-sector MFP fell 0.6% in 2024 and was **0.7% below its 2019 level**, with capital deepening at −0.9 ([ONS, May 2025](https://www.ons.gov.uk/economy/economicoutputandproductivity/productivitymeasures/bulletins/growthaccountingmultifactorproductivityestimatesuk/octobertodecember2024)). EU KLEMS/INTANProd only runs to 2021 ([EUKLEMS-INTANProd](https://euklems-intanprod-llee.luiss.it/)), so post-2021 European growth accounting has to come from the [OECD Compendium of Productivity Indicators 2026](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-compendium-of-productivity-indicators-2026_734a5e68-en/full-report.html). The right framing for the paper is therefore not "Australia uniquely failed". It is that **Australia failed alongside most of the advanced world while the US broke away**, and that Australia had specific extra drags.

### Mismeasurement is real for Australia, but it is not about digital goods

The canonical US mismeasurement tests transfer well. Byrne, Fernald and Reinsdorf find the post-2004 US slowdown mainly real, and correcting IT hardware prices makes it *larger* ([Byrne, Fernald & Reinsdorf 2016](https://www.brookings.edu/bpea-articles/does-the-united-states-have-a-productivity-slowdown-or-a-measurement-problem/)). Syverson shows that slowdown size across countries is unrelated to ICT intensity, and that the implied missing digital output is implausibly large ([Syverson 2017](https://www.nber.org/papers/w21974)). The Australian evidence fits that pattern. Information media and telecoms (+4.1%) and finance (+3.1%) were among the *strongest* MFP performers in 2024–25 ([ABS 2026](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/industry/industry-overview/estimates-industry-multifactor-productivity/latest-release)), which is inconsistent with a story in which digital mismeasurement hides growth.

Australia's material measurement problems lie elsewhere. The first is non-market output. On the PC's quality-adjusted measure, healthcare productivity grew about **3% a year** across disease groups covering about a third of the sector ([PC 2024, Advances in measuring healthcare productivity](https://assets.pc.gov.au/research/completed/measuring-healthcare-productivity/measuring-healthcare-productivity.pdf)). The second is labour-quality interpolation. The third is mining and electricity output concepts, which omit depletion, reliability and emissions. The fourth is hours revisions and PPP vintages. An article in the *Economic and Labour Relations Review* argues that the input-based convention biases non-market productivity toward zero, which turns a measurement convention into an apparent public-sector drag ([ELRR](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/the-economic-and-labour-relations-review/article/is-the-public-sector-dragging-down-living-standards/999E04A996D00349F37F9166D904BF5B)). Quiggin's earlier critique, that the 1990s "surge" partly reflected unmeasured work intensity and arbitrary cycle dating ([Quiggin, ANU Press](https://press-files.anu.edu.au/downloads/press/n12514/pdf/ch07.pdf)), is a reminder that cycle dating is a real choice. The paper should present results for alternative start years.

## More hours, more people and a bigger care economy explain the arithmetic

### A record surge in hours, mostly from employment rather than longer days

Between March 2020 and March 2025, Australian **hours worked rose 12.8% and employed persons 13.2%**, so hours per worker were roughly flat ([ABS, Insights into the Australian labour market](https://www.abs.gov.au/articles/insights-australian-labour-market)). The PC describes "a record surge in hours worked between September 2021 and June 2023". It attributes the surge to pent-up demand, record participation and migrant inflows, and judges total migrant supply since the pandemic to be "broadly as expected" ([PC, Productivity before and after COVID-19](https://assets.pc.gov.au/2025-10/covid-productivity-research-paper_0.pdf)).

Canada shows the contrast. Its population grew **3.2% in 2023**, about three times the 1990s pace, yet real GDP per capita was 2.5% below its pre-pandemic level ([McCormack & Wang, StatCan 2024](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/pub/36-28-0001/2024004/article/00001-eng.htm)). Population outran employment, so hours per head fell. Australia's employment rate rose instead. The euro area shows a third pattern: employment rose while average hours per worker fell, so productivity per worker fell about 0.8% while productivity per hour *rose* about 0.6% ([ECB Blog, May 2024](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/press/blog/date/2024/html/ecb.blog20240506~f9c0c49ff7.en.html)). Cross-country comparisons in the paper must therefore be per hour, not per worker.

### COVID composition effects largely washed out by 2022

Most of the 2020 productivity spike was compositional, across all the countries studied. In the PC's shift-share, about **86% of the change from December 2019 to December 2020** came from between-industry reallocation as low-productivity hours dropped out. But the **6.1% fall from March 2022 to June 2023 was only 9% between-industry** ([PC 2025](https://assets.pc.gov.au/2025-10/covid-productivity-research-paper_0.pdf)). The BLS ([Stewart 2022](https://www.bls.gov/osmr/research-papers/2022/pdf/ec220010.pdf)), the San Francisco Fed ([FRBSF 2024](https://www.frbsf.org/research-and-insights/publications/economic-letter/2024/11/productivity-during-and-since-pandemic/)) and the ONS ([ONS 2021](https://www.ons.gov.uk/employmentandlabourmarket/peopleinwork/labourproductivity/articles/ukproductivityintroduction/julytoseptember2021)) document the same mechanism, which the ILO says was particularly visible in the US and Canada ([ILOSTAT blog](https://ilostat.ilo.org/blog/why-would-labour-productivity-surge-during-a-pandemic/)). For a 2019-to-2025 endpoint comparison these effects broadly net out, and the PC concludes productivity has "stabilised at its pre-COVID-19 level".

The PC and RBA disagree on one point the paper should flag. The PC names less-experienced entrants as one of two drivers of the 2022–23 decline. The RBA's direct labour-quality measure attributes only **about 0.4 pp of the 3.5% fall** to declining quality, and finds "little to no evidence" that younger or less-skilled entrants explain the weakness ([Bruno, Hambur & Wang 2025](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/bulletin/2025/jul/a-closer-to-real-time-labour-quality-index.html)). The RBA estimate is the more direct measurement and should carry more weight.

### Migration diluted capital temporarily; the composition channel is better evidenced

Official work confirms a *transitional* capital-shallowing mechanism. The PC finds capital growth "still insufficient to match increases in hours worked – a function of long term investment weakness in Australia rather than a COVID-19-specific phenomenon" ([PC 2025](https://assets.pc.gov.au/2025-10/covid-productivity-research-paper_0.pdf)). The Bank of Canada explicitly modelled **negative capital deepening in 2024** because strong population growth raised trend labour input faster than capital ([BoC Staff Analytical Note 2024-11](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/2024/04/staff-analytical-note-2024-11/)). With immigration now lower, it projects trend labour productivity of about **1.4% a year** ([BoC MPR April 2026](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/publications/mpr/mpr-2026-04-29/appendix/)). In neither country does rigorous work make migration the *primary* cause. Investment per worker in Canada has fallen since 2006, to about 15% below that year's level by 2021 ([StatCan 2024](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/pub/36-28-0001/2024004/article/00001-eng.htm)). Australian capital per worker was flat for about five years before the pandemic. Both trends predate the migration surge. Strong causal claims, such as the claim that "immigration is wrecking productivity", come from advocacy outlets and misstate the RBA ([MacroBusiness 2025](https://www.macrobusiness.com.au/2025/03/rba-admits-immigration-is-wrecking-australias-productivity/)).

The better-evidenced channel is visa *composition*. e61's microdata show skilled-visa holders concentrated in higher-productivity firms, and student and working-holiday holders in lower-productivity firms ([e61, Misallocated migrants](https://e61.in/misallocated-migrants-immigration-and-firm-productivity-in-australia/)). This is a misallocation channel, not a dilution channel, and the paper should keep the two distinct. Treasury's Intergenerational Report migration sensitivity explicitly "does not take into account any productivity effect" of migration ([Treasury 2021 IGR](https://treasury.gov.au/sites/default/files/2021-06/p2021_182464.pdf)). No official estimate of net overseas migration's effect on capital per hour exists for 2022–25.

### The care economy absorbed half of all new hours and subtracts about 0.3 pp a year

This is Australia's most distinctive compositional feature. Over March 2020 to March 2025, the non-market sector accounted for about **55% of filled-jobs growth and 52.6% of hours growth**. Health care and social assistance supplied about 70% of that share, with its hours up **39.0%** ([ABS 2025](https://www.abs.gov.au/articles/insights-australian-labour-market)). The RBA estimates the rising non-market share lowered economy-wide labour productivity growth by **about 0.3 pp a year over 2017/18–2023/24, double the drag of the preceding decade** ([Plumb 2025](https://www.rba.gov.au/speeches/2025/sp-so-2025-02-27.html)). The PC finds the care economy contributed **1.1 pp of the 6.1% fall** in 2022–23. It traces the growth to NDIS rollout, post-Royal Commission aged-care standards and childcare subsidies, and warns that if quality is mismeasured, "the post-pandemic decline in measured productivity is exaggerated" ([PC 2025](https://assets.pc.gov.au/2025-10/covid-productivity-research-paper_0.pdf)).

Scaled over six years, this drag alone accounts for about 0.3 of the roughly 1.1 pp gap between recent growth and Australia's historical 1.3% average (our derivation). Because much of it is a measurement convention applied to policy-driven demand, the paper should report **market-sector-excluding-mining productivity (about 1.0% a year over 2017/18–2023/24)** alongside the aggregate.

### France is the closest European analogue; labour hoarding is not the Australian story

The Banque de France decomposes France's 8.5% shortfall in productivity per head relative to its 2010–19 trend. It attributes **1.2 pp to subsidised apprenticeships, 1.4 pp to workforce composition, 0.4 pp to COVID effects on potential, and 1.7 pp to labour hoarding**, with about 3.6 pp unexplained. It describes much of the loss as higher "employment intensity" of growth rather than lost potential ([Devulder et al. 2024](https://www.banque-france.fr/en/publications-and-statistics/publications/explaining-productivity-losses-observed-france-pre-covid-period)). Both France and Australia had policy-driven growth in low-measured-productivity employment: apprenticeships in France, care funding in Australia. The European Stability Mechanism (ESM) estimates German employment about 7% and French employment about 5% above activity-consistent levels in mid-2024, which makes hoarding a major euro-area factor concentrated in industry ([ESM Blog 2024](https://www.esm.europa.eu/blog/labour-hoarding-euro-areas-achilles-heel)). Australia's flat hours per worker and record employment rate look like strong demand plus supply expansion, not hoarding, and no RBA or ABS analysis frames it as hoarding. One related Australian policy did matter: the PC argues JobKeeper "limited worker mobility, firm entry and exit" and may have prevented the productivity-enhancing reallocation seen in the US ([PC 2025](https://assets.pc.gov.au/2025-10/covid-productivity-research-paper_0.pdf)).

## Mining, electricity and construction subtract from MFP for different reasons

### Mining: depletion and lags then, weather and grades now

Mining MFP fell **3.3% in 2024–25**, its fifth consecutive decline, which the ABS attributes to weather disruptions. Mining's direct contribution to market-sector labour productivity was **−1.0 pp**, partly offset by **+0.7 pp from hours reallocation**, because mining's labour productivity level is high ([ABS 2026](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/industry/industry-overview/estimates-industry-multifactor-productivity/latest-release)). Cumulative mining MFP from 2001–02 to 2023–24 was **−34%** ([PC June 2026 chart data](https://assets.pc.gov.au/2026-08/quarterly-bulletin-june-2026-chart-data.xlsx)). Topp, Soames, Parham and Bloch showed that the **24% fall over 2000–01 to 2006–07** was driven by declining yields and by production lags after the investment surge. Once these are removed, "other factors are estimated to have raised mining MFP by 8 per cent" ([Topp et al. 2008](https://assets.pc.gov.au/research/supporting/mining-productivity/mining-productivity.pdf)). The ABS's experimental resource-adjusted mining MFP, which includes subsoil assets in capital services, "significantly moderates" the measured decline ([ABS 2013–14 feature article](https://www.abs.gov.au/ausstats/abs@.nsf/Lookup/5260.0.55.002Feature+Article12013-14)). Whether that series is still published is unconfirmed. The paper should not attribute all post-2019 mining weakness to depletion: recent years combine weather, falling grades and capacity still ramping up.

Canada shows the same price-induced extensive-margin mechanism. Oil-and-gas labour productivity fell **8.2% a year between 2000 and 2007**, yet the industry contributed positively to aggregate productivity through price and share effects ([Sharpe & Bradley, CSLS 2009](https://econpapers.repec.org/paper/slsresrep/0908.htm)). High prices made oil sands viable even though each barrel required more inputs ([Government of Alberta 2012](https://open.alberta.ca/dataset/12c02d5e-12ce-4751-a4f4-3d2d12f09b16/resource/511df1f7-52b9-4f0a-94f4-cc498aee731e/download/6582918-2012-alberta-productivity-story-1997-2010-2012-11-14.pdf.)). Norway's statistical convention of separating "mainland" from petroleum activity, in which mainland market productivity growth fell from about 3% to 0.8% a year after 2005 ([NOU 2015:1](https://files.nettsteder.regjeringen.no/wpuploads01/blogs.dir/56/files/2015/04/summary_firstreport_productivityCommission.pdf)), suggests a useful presentational choice for Australia: report MFP excluding mining and utilities.

### Electricity: a transition build-phase that MFP reads as failure

Utilities had the worst cumulative MFP of any industry from 2001–02 to 2023–24, at **−35%**. In the PC's experimental electricity estimate, **the capital stock rose 126% while output rose only 14%**. Each $1 of output now needs about $10 of capital, against just over $2 economy-wide ([PC Quarterly bulletin, June 2026](https://assets.pc.gov.au/2026-06/quarterly-bulletin-june-2026.pdf)). About **37% of investment since 2017–18** has gone into renewable generation and storage. Lower capacity factors (19% for utility solar against 89% for new black coal, using CSIRO GenCost assumptions) mechanically raise capital per unit of output. The PC explicitly compares the sector to "the 'investment phase' of a mining boom".

Adjustments change the picture substantially. With AER quality adjustment, distribution productivity fell **8% instead of 24%** and transmission **15% instead of 26%** over 2006–07 to 2023–24. Valuing the 30% fall in emissions at $67 a tonne turns a 32% fall in electricity productivity into a **23% fall** over 2001–02 to 2022–23. The two adjustments cover different scopes and cannot be summed. The 2008–14 collapse in electricity MFP coincides with the network investment surge, and the post-2017 decline with renewables, so the two phases need separate treatment. Policy decisions will set how long the build phase lasts. The PC estimates that a one-year acceleration of approvals for wind and transmission could cut electricity bills by **7% over a decade**, and that federal rather than state renewable targets would cut costs by 8% ([PC June 2026](https://assets.pc.gov.au/2026-06/quarterly-bulletin-june-2026.pdf)).

Germany shows a different mechanism with the same symptom. The IMF attributes about 60% of Germany's downturn to lower potential and 40% to the cycle, with productivity falling especially in industry and construction. The 2022 energy-price shock amplified these structural problems but did not cause them ([Kemp, IMF WP/26/112](https://ideas.repec.org/p/imf/imfwpa/2026-112.html)). Energy prices 20% above the 2018–19 average reduce German potential output by about **1.2%** ([IMF SIP 2023/059](https://www.imf.org/en/Publications/selected-issues-papers/Issues/2023/07/24/Impact-of-High-Energy-Prices-on-Germanys-Potential-Output-536837)). Germany suffered a *price* shock to an energy-importing industrial base. Australia is undergoing a *capital-deepening* transition in an energy-exporting economy. Both depress measured MFP.

### Construction: physical productivity has halved, and the transition competes for the same workers

The PC finds that dwellings completed per hour worked fell **53%** over about 30 years and value-based labour productivity fell 12%, while economy-wide labour productivity rose 49%. Detached-house productivity has fallen while higher-density building has improved. Only 35% of construction firms are innovation-active, and the top four firms held just 12% of the market in 2017 ([PC 2025, Housing construction productivity](https://assets.pc.gov.au/research/completed/housing-construction/housing-construction.pdf)). Construction MFP fell **2.8% in 2024–25** ([ABS 2026](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/industry/industry-overview/estimates-industry-multifactor-productivity/latest-release)). Critics argue that the physical-unit measure overstates the decline because dwellings have become larger and better ([Murray, Fresh Economic Thinking](https://www.fresheconomicthinking.com/p/despite-what-youve-heard-we-dont)). Even so, the contrast between detached and higher-density building points to regulation and site constraints rather than technology. Because new generation and transmission is counted as *construction* output, the roughly $21.6b of energy-related construction in 2023–24 competes directly with housing for the same labour ([PC June 2026](https://assets.pc.gov.au/2026-06/quarterly-bulletin-june-2026.pdf)).

### Southeast Asia: Singapore's frontier gains contrast with resource-economy stagnation

On ILO modelled estimates (paper's extraction from [ILOSTAT](https://sdmx.ilo.org/rest/data/ILO,DF_GDP_2HRW_NOC_NB,1.0/)), Southeast Asia splits clearly. **Singapore grew 2.11% a year over 2019–25 and stood at about 140% of Australia's GDP per hour in 2025.** Indonesia grew 2.62%, Vietnam 4.25%, Malaysia 1.54% and the Philippines 1.61%. Thailand (0.42%) and Brunei (0.06%) stagnated. The ILO series puts Australia's own growth at 0.84%, so these comparisons are valid only within the ILO source. Brunei is the clearest resource-economy analogue. Its GDP per hour is roughly equal to Australia's, it has had no growth, and the IMF calls its diversification challenge "complex" given declining hydrocarbons ([IMF Article IV 2024](https://www.imf.org/en/Publications/CR/Issues/2024/09/24/Brunei-Darussalam-2024-Article-IV-Consultation-Press-Release-and-Staff-Report-555551)). Thailand's productivity growth fell from **4.8% (2010–15) to 2.1% (2015–23)** ([OECD Economic Survey of Thailand 2025](https://issuu.com/oecd.publishing/docs/oecd_economic_surveys_thailand_2025)). The World Bank says its growth model has stalled and calls for services opening and lower competition barriers ([Nation Thailand, reporting World Bank](https://www.nationthailand.com/business/economy/40056544)). Indonesia's nickel downstreaming, achieved through export bans, raised domestic value added, but the IMF criticised its lack of cost-benefit analysis ([Jakarta Post](https://www.thejakartapost.com/opinion/2023/07/12/analysis-ri-adamant-on-upholding-downstream-policy-despite-imf-criticism)). Work-in-progress World Bank research hypothesises hidden allocative costs in downstream industries ([World Bank slides 2025](https://thedocs.worldbank.org/en/doc/6c4802573b0952b66e1106612422f12a-0050012025/related/4A-SlidesIndonesiaExportBanNickel.pdf)). This bears on Australia's Future Made in Australia and green-metals ambitions. The notes contain no sourced evidence for Malaysia beyond the ILO series.

## Weak investment, fading dynamism and slow diffusion are the deeper structural story

### Non-mining investment never recovered from the GFC

PC commissioners state that **non-mining investment has fallen 3.2 percentage points as a share of GDP since the GFC**, and that capital deepening, historically Australia's main source of productivity growth, "has stalled" ([Robson & Sterland, PC 2025](https://www.pc.gov.au/media-speeches/articles/reform-company-tax)). The PC's December 2025 *Creating a more dynamic and resilient economy* report finds business investment has "fallen notably" over the decade ([PC five pillars](https://www.pc.gov.au/inquiries-and-research/five-productivity-inquiries/meeting-productivity-challenge)). The RBA's earlier structural account attributes part of the long decline to a shift toward less capital-intensive services ([RBA Bulletin, Dec 2014](https://rba.gov.au/publications/bulletin/2014/dec/1.html)). Recent capex strength is concentrated in renewables and data centres.

Intangibles are probably where the US gap is widest. US intangible investment reached **15.6% of GDP in 2025, against 10.3% for tangibles**, and grew about three times as fast over 2015–25 ([WIPO, World Intangible Investment Highlights 2026](https://www.wipo.int/web-publications/world-intangible-investment-highlights-2026/en/key-trends-and-insights.html)). The latest sourced Australian estimate is dated: about $57b of market-sector intangibles in 2005–06, roughly 80% of which was not capitalised ([PC, Barnes & McClure 2009](https://www.pc.gov.au/inquiries-and-research/intangible-investment/)). The fall of more than 40% in R&D's share of GFCF points the same way. Canada is the cautionary comparator. Fraser estimates Canadian business investment per worker fell from **87.3% of the US level in 2014 to 54.0% in 2024** ([Fraser Institute 2026](https://www.fraserinstitute.org/studies/comparing-business-investment-per-worker-canada-and-united-states-2007-2024)), and C.D. Howe finds capital per worker falling since 2015 ([Robson & Bafale 2025](https://cdhowe.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/Commentary_699.pdf)). Canada combined fast labour-force growth with falling capital per worker, which makes it the closest analogue to Australian capital shallowing.

### Competition: modest but material, at 1–3% of GDP

Treasury and RBA firm-level work using BLADE finds a consistent pattern. The top four firms' share of 4-digit industry sales rose about **2 points to 43%** between 2001–02 and 2018–19. Incumbency in the top four rose from 71% to 75%. Markups rose about **6% between 2003–04 and 2016–17**, more than twice as fast in digitally intensive sectors. Reduced dynamic allocation cut annual labour productivity growth by about **0.1 pp, roughly one-fifth of the slowdown since 2012** ([Day et al., Treasury Round Up 2022](https://treasury.gov.au/sites/default/files/2022-10/p2022-325290-productivity-growth.pdf)). The rise is within-firm rather than a shift toward superstar firms. The journal version puts the markup rise at about 5% ([Hambur 2023, *Economic Record*](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:ecorec:v:99:y:2023:i:324:p:32-57)). Hambur and Freestone calibrate the Edmond–Midrigan–Xu model to Australian microdata. They find that if competition had stayed at mid-2000s levels, productivity and GDP would be **1–3% higher** from better allocation alone ([RBA RDP 2025-05](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/rdp/2025/2025-05.html)).

The sceptical case deserves space. Stephen King argues the measured concentration rises "are not huge" and that competition's role in falling dynamism is "far from clear", since churn also falls when firms face fewer shocks ([King, Monash Lens 2023](https://lens.monash.edu/yes-australian-businesses-have-become-less-dynamic-but-there-are-bigger-reasons-for-our-sliding-productivity-growth/)). Hambur and La Cava also find retail recently became *more* competitive ([EconBiz record](https://www.econbiz.de/Record/business-concentration-and-mark-ups-in-the-retail-trade-sector-hambur-jonathan/10012270355)). The fair reading is that competition is a material but not dominant explanation. Mandatory, suspensory ACCC merger notification started on **1 January 2026** ([White & Case](https://www.whitecase.com/insight-alert/transition-mandatory-merger-control-australia-key-parameters-new-regime)), too recently for any outcome evidence.

### Diffusion to laggards, not frontier innovation, is the core failure

Andrews, Criscuolo and Gal showed across 23 countries that global frontier firms kept accelerating in the 2000s while laggards stalled, with diffusion slower where product markets were less competitive ([Andrews, Criscuolo & Gal 2016](https://www.brookings.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/09/wp24_andrews-et-al_final.pdf)). Australia fits this pattern strongly. **Global frontier labour productivity rose 60% between 2002 and 2016, against 25% for Australian frontier firms and 15% for Australian laggards.** The convergence speed fell about 9%, and BLADE firm entry fell from **14.5% to 11.5%**. Declines in dynamism and competitive pressure account for **half to three-quarters of the slowdown in convergence** ([Andrews, Hambur, Hansell & Wheeler 2022](https://treasury.gov.au/sites/default/files/2022-02/p2022-243535.pdf)). Capital reallocation toward productive firms has also slowed ([Hambur & Andrews, RBA RDP 2023-03](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/rdp/2023/2023-03/full.html)).

Labour mobility has declined as well. Job switching fell from about 11.3% in the mid-2000s to 8.5% in 2019, and recovered only to 9.5% in 2023. Switchers move to firms about 13% more productive, and e61 attributes about **one-quarter of the slowdown** to reduced reallocation ([HR Leader, reporting e61](https://www.hrleader.com.au/business/24811-why-job-mobility-could-be-the-key-to-the-productivity-slowdown)). The 2022 mobility spike did not reduce match quality ([RBA Bulletin, July 2024](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/bulletin/2024/jul/skills-match-quality-following-the-covid-19-pandemic.html)). The US moved the other way. Decker and Haltiwanger document a genuine surge in employer business formation after 2020, which shifted activity toward young firms and was tied to quits rather than layoffs ([Decker & Haltiwanger 2023](https://www.brookings.edu/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/Decker-Haltiwanger_16820-BPEA-FA23_WEB.pdf)). Recent Australian entry, by contrast, has been mostly non-employing businesses (King 2023). Different reallocation regimes are a credible, though unquantified, part of the post-2020 divergence.

The estimates in this section (one-fifth from markups, one-quarter from mobility, half to three-quarters of the convergence slowdown) come from different methods and windows, and they are not additive.

### AI: Australia is adopting fast from a low base, while the US payoff is still mostly elsewhere

Australian business AI use jumped from **1% in 2022–23 to 12% in 2024–25** on ABS measures ([ABS, Characteristics of Australian Business 2024–25](https://www.abs.gov.au/media-centre/media-releases/business-adoption-artificial-intelligence-accelerates-2024-25)). Industry surveys report far higher figures on non-comparable definitions. In the US, the Kansas City Fed finds productivity well above its pre-pandemic trend since late 2022, with faster growth in high-AI-adoption industries. It concludes that AI explains little of the aggregate shift and that adoption is "still spreading" ([Çakır Melek & Miller 2026](https://ideas.repec.org/a/fip/fedkeb/102427.html)). Micro gains are large: generative AI raised customer-support resolutions per hour by **14% on average and about 34% for novices** ([Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:nbr:nberwo:31161)). Macro bounds are modest: Acemoglu finds **no more than about 0.66% TFP over ten years** ([Acemoglu 2025](https://www.nber.org/papers/w32487)). The productivity J-curve implies measured TFP first falls while firms build complementary intangibles ([Brynjolfsson, Rock & Syverson 2021](https://doi.org/10.1257/mac.20180386)). That makes Australia's weak intangible investment the binding complement. The PC's central scenario of **A$116b of extra GDP over a decade** is a scenario estimate, built on an "enable first, regulate carefully" approach ([PC Inquiry Report 111](https://assets.pc.gov.au/2025-12/data-digital_0.pdf)). Singapore takes the contrasting route of direct subsidy, through its **S$150m Enterprise Compute Initiative** ([DISG](https://www.disg.gov.sg/enterprise-compute-initiative/)).

## Policy lessons: implementation, not diagnosis, is the binding constraint

The evidence points to eight lessons.

**Measurement.** Australia should change how it reads its own statistics before it changes policy. The quality-adjusted health productivity measure (+3% a year) and the emissions- and reliability-adjusted electricity measures show that today's headline aggregates combine real stagnation with conventions that penalise exactly the sectors that policy is expanding. Routinely publishing market-sector-excluding-mining figures, quality-adjusted non-market output (following the UK post-Atkinson approach) and natural-resource-adjusted mining MFP would sharpen accountability without excusing the market-sector failure.

**Investment and tax.** International practice is moving toward cash-flow tax treatment of investment. The US made 100% bonus depreciation permanent in the One Big Beautiful Bill Act of 2025 ([CPA Ontario](https://www.cpaontario.ca/insights/blog/what-the-one-big-beautiful-bill-act-means-for-canadian-tax)). In September 2026 Canada proposed a permanent Productivity Mega Deduction. The government claims it would lower Canada's marginal effective tax rate on new investment from 13.0% to 6.4%, at a cost of about C$36b over five years ([Finance Canada 2026](https://www.canada.ca/en/department-finance/news/2026/09/government-of-canada-introduces-new-productivity-mega-deduction-to-boost-canadas-advantage-as-the-most-competitive-g7-country-for-new-business-inve.html)). The PC's hybrid goes in the same direction: 20% for firms under A$1b, 28% above, plus a 5% net cash-flow tax. It has not been legislated and faces professional-body opposition ([Accounting Times](https://www.accountingtimes.com.au/tax/industry-bodies-continue-backlash-against-experimental-net-cashflow-tax)). Without reform, Australia's relative tax position on investment worsens by default.

**Competition and dynamism.** The 1990s offer the most usable precedent. The PC's 2005 review credited National Competition Policy and related reforms with raising GDP by about 2.5% ([PC 2005](https://www.pc.gov.au/inquiries-and-research/national-competition-policy/report)), and its key design feature was paying states for independently assessed reform. The November 2024 revival restores that model through a **$900m National Productivity Fund** ([Treasurer media release](https://ministers.treasury.gov.au/ministers/jim-chalmers-2022/media-releases/national-agreement-revitalise-competition-consumers)), with modelled gains of **$26–45b (1.0–1.7% of GDP)** ([PC NCP modelling 2024](https://www.pc.gov.au/inquiries-and-research/competition-analysis/report)). Today the gains lie in services, care, planning and occupational licensing rather than government-owned networks, and those gains are harder to estimate in advance and to attribute afterwards.

**Energy transition.** Faster approvals and technology-neutral, federal targets shorten the low-MFP build phase.

**Housing.** Planning reform that tilts the mix toward medium density would lift measured construction productivity.

**Migration.** Visa composition and skills use matter more than headline numbers.

**AI.** Policy should focus on diffusion to laggards and complementary intangibles rather than the frontier.

**Implementation.** This is the hardest lesson. The PC's 2023 inquiry made 71 recommendations, and a source involved in preparing the report said essentially none had been acted on ([ABC News 2025](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2025-03-31/federal-election-housing-productivity-bandaid-solutions-budget/105098402)). After the 47 recommendations of December 2025, the government claims to be "progressing 13 of the 17 reform areas" ([PC Sept 2026](https://www.pc.gov.au/ongoing/productivity-insights/update-september-2026/)). Note that these are two different inquiries, not competing counts. The EU shows what happens when diagnosis lacks enforcement. Only about **11% of Draghi's 383 recommendations were fully implemented after a year, and about 15% by January 2026**, on EPIC's tracker ([Eunews](https://www.eunews.it/en/2025/09/03/nestoras-epic-one-year-after-the-draghi-report-a-sense-of-urgency-is-still-lacking)). US industrial policy (CHIPS, IRA) has raised announced manufacturing construction but has not yet shown aggregate productivity gains. The new industrial-policy literature supports export-disciplined, time-limited support rather than open-ended production credits ([Juhász, Lane & Rodrik 2024](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-economics-081023-024638)). That is relevant to Future Made in Australia.

## Open research questions the paper can credibly address

Seven questions are both open and tractable with Australian data.

**How much of the stagnation survives quality-adjusted non-market output?** Non-market productivity is now below its March 2007 level. Applying outcome-based deflators (QALYs for health, value-added measures for education) to ABS non-market output, benchmarked against the ONS, would directly test whether the 0.3 pp a year drag is economic or statistical.

**What explains the gap between OECD whole-economy MFP and ABS market-sector MFP?** The figures are −0.12 pp and about +0.3% a year over overlapping windows. A formal bridge would decompose the difference into coverage, GDP versus GVA, calendar versus financial year, labour-input basis and vintage. No published source does this.

**Did migration-driven hours growth reduce capital per hour in 2022–25, separately from pre-existing investment weakness?** BLADE-linked migrant employment shares by firm, combined with firm capital stocks, could separate dilution from visa-composition misallocation. Treasury's Intergenerational Report currently assumes no productivity effect.

**Is there an AI J-curve in Australian firm data?** The redesigned biennial Business Characteristics Survey linked to BLADE allows event-study estimates of how firm TFP evolves after AI and intangible adoption. These would test whether the PC's A$116b scenario fits observed lags. No Australian firm-level causal AI estimate exists yet.

**How much of mining and electricity MFP decline is depletion, weather, build-phase lag and unmeasured quality?** The Topp et al. decomposition is nearly two decades old and needs updating. The ABS resource-adjusted mining series and the PC's emissions-adjusted electricity measure provide starting points, and applying the method of Brandt, Schreyer and Zipperer (2017) on natural capital across Australia, Canada and Norway would add a comparative dimension.

**How much of the Australia–US divergence after 2020 do the different reallocation regimes explain?** JobKeeper-style retention contrasts with the US surge in quits and business formation. Comparable OECD DynEmp entry series and job-to-job flows would allow a cross-country test that no source in this evidence base attempts.

**Do PC recommendations get implemented, and does implementation predict sectoral productivity?** A coded tracker of every PC recommendation since 2005, modelled on EPIC's Draghi Observatory, would turn the political economy of reform into data. A related question is how ex-ante modelling (the 2024 NCP estimate of $26–45b) compares with ex-post outcomes (the 2005 estimate of 2.5% of GDP).

Several questions remain where the evidence base itself is thin. The ICP 2017 versus ICP 2021 PPP effect on the Australia–US level gap has not been computed. The gap between LFS and Labour Account hours over 2019–25 has not been quantified. No harmonised AI-adoption rates exist across Australia, Canada, the EU and Singapore. Malaysian resource-dependence evidence is missing. No Australian marginal effective tax rate estimate exists for 2026.

## Conclusion

Australia's post-2019 stagnation is best understood as the moment several slow-moving problems stopped being hidden. The mining boom, then the terms of trade, then rapid population and hours growth had each kept income per person rising while output per hour lost momentum. Once hours growth is netted out, there is almost nothing left. The comparison with Canada and Europe matters as much as the comparison with the US. It shows that the forces behind the US acceleration (rapid capital and intangible deepening, a reallocation shock that pushed resources into young firms, and an early lead in diffusing digital technology) were largely absent from every comparator, not just Australia. What is specifically Australian is the stack of extra drags: an input-measured care economy expanding by policy design, resource and electricity sectors in capital-heavy phases, and two decades of weaker entry and slower catch-up to the frontier.

For the paper, this suggests a reframing. The question should not be why Australia's productivity collapsed, because measured MFP has been weak since the mid-2000s. It should be why Australia's growth model drifted from capital deepening and diffusion toward labour-input expansion, and whether its statistics are good enough to tell real stagnation from statistical convention in the sectors growing fastest. The most defensible headline is a range, not a point. Hourly productivity growth was roughly zero on whole-economy OECD data, about 0.2–0.5% a year in the market sector depending on start year, and about 1.0% in the market sector excluding mining. Every version is far below the US and Australia's own history. The most valuable empirical contributions are the bridges between these measures and firm-level tests of the reallocation and diffusion channels.
