Working More, Producing Little: Australia’s Post-2019 Productivity Stagnation in International Perspective

Charley Peng

Working draft, November 2026

Abstract

Australian GDP per hour worked grew 0.10% a year over 2019–2025 on OECD whole-economy data, against 1.88% in the United States, 0.44% in Canada and 0.53% in the EU-27. Australia’s output per hour fell from 95% of the US level in 2000 to 81% in 2025 (current OECD vintage). We reconcile these figures with Australian Bureau of Statistics (ABS) and Productivity Commission (PC) market-sector statistics, decompose growth into multifactor productivity (MFP) and capital deepening, and compare Australia with the US, Canada, Europe and Southeast Asia. Three findings stand out. First, Australian living standards rose about 1% a year per person since 2019 almost entirely because hours worked per head rose 5.3%; output per hour contributed essentially nothing. Second, Australia’s stagnation is not unique. Canada, the UK and continental Europe also recorded weak MFP and capital deepening, so the US is the outlier. Third, four features add to Australia’s weakness: an expanding care economy whose output is measured by its inputs, which subtracts about 0.3 percentage points a year; long measured MFP declines in mining and electricity tied to depletion and the energy-transition build phase; a non-mining investment share 3.2 points of GDP below its pre-GFC level; and two decades of declining competition, firm entry and diffusion of technology to firms behind the frontier. Measurement corrections shrink, but do not eliminate, the problem. We argue the binding constraint is implementing reforms that have already been recommended, and we set out seven tractable research questions, starting with a formal bridge between OECD and ABS productivity measures.

1 Introduction

For three decades, output per hour worked was the main source of Australian prosperity. Since 2019 it has done almost no work. On OECD whole-economy data, Australian GDP per hour grew 0.10% a year between 2019 and 2025. Over the same years, US output per hour rose from US$76.6 to US$85.6 (constant 2020 PPP), an average of 1.88% a year (OECD, 2026). The Reserve Bank of Australia (RBA) now describes labour productivity as “currently sitting at around 2019 levels”. In August 2025 it cut its trend productivity assumption from 1.0% to 0.7% (Australia, 2026). The most recent data have not turned. Labour productivity fell 0.2% over the year to June 2026, because hours worked grew faster than output (P. Commission, 2026c).

This paper makes three contributions. First, it reconciles the headline international statistics with the domestic statistics most often quoted in Australian debate. The two give materially different numbers: about 0.1% a year for the whole economy on OECD calendar-year data, and about 0.2–0.5% a year for the ABS market sector, depending on the start year (Statistics, 2026b). We show that the difference comes from six identifiable sources and that every one of them pushes the OECD measure down. We also show that the gap between Australia and the United States is robust across sources, even though Australia’s own growth rate is not.

Second, the paper places Australia in a broader comparison than is usual. Most Australian commentary benchmarks against the United States. We add Canada, a resource exporter whose population growth is also driven by immigration; Europe, where the energy shock hit and labour hoarding and subsidised employment are documented; and Southeast Asia, where Singapore operates at the productivity frontier while resource-dependent economies have stalled. That wider comparison changes the diagnosis. Australia failed alongside most of the advanced world, while the United States broke away.

Third, the paper separates the parts of the stagnation that are common to many countries from those specific to Australia, and real stagnation from artefacts of statistical convention. Four Australian features stand out. The first is a care economy that grew by policy design and whose output is measured by its inputs. The second is mining and electricity, both in capital-heavy phases. The third is a non-mining investment share that never recovered from the global financial crisis. The fourth is two decades of weakening competition, firm entry and diffusion of technology to firms behind the frontier.

The paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 describes the data and the growth-accounting framework and reconciles the main sources. Section 3 sets out the stylised facts. Section 4 decomposes growth into MFP and capital deepening. Section 5 examines hours, migration and the composition of employment. Section 6 turns to mining, electricity and construction. Section 7 considers investment, competition, dynamism and technology diffusion. Section 8 draws the international comparisons together. Section 9 discusses policy and section 10 sets out a research agenda. Section 11 concludes.

2 Data and framework

2.1 Sources

We use four sources. All of the datasets we compiled are published with full schemas in the accompanying repository.

  1. Productivity Commission chart data (September 2026 update; June 2026 quarterly bulletin). These give quarterly, seasonally adjusted labour productivity indices for the whole economy, the market sector and the non-market sector from June 2004 to June 2026. They also give cumulative MFP growth by industry over 2001–02 to 2023–24 (P. Commission, 2026c, 2026b).
  2. OECD Productivity Database (SDMX dataflow DSD_PDB@DF_PDB v2.0). This gives GDP per hour worked in constant 2020 PPP US dollars, GDP per capita, hours worked per head of population, and growth-accounting contributions of MFP and capital deepening to labour productivity growth, for the whole economy in calendar years 2000–2025 (OECD, 2026).
  3. ABS Estimates of Industry Multifactor Productivity (February 2026 release). These are market-sector MFP, labour productivity and capital services on a financial-year basis (Statistics, 2026b).
  4. ILOSTAT modelled estimates of output per hour worked (constant 2021 PPP dollars), used only for Southeast Asia, which the OECD does not cover (Organization, 2026). Years after 2025 are projections and are excluded from all calculations.

2.2 Growth-accounting framework

Under constant returns and competitive factor markets, log labour productivity growth decomposes as

Δln⁡(YH)=s‾KΔln⁡(KH)⏟capital deepening+Δln⁡A⏟MFP,\Delta \ln\!\left(\frac{Y}{H}\right) = \underbrace{\bar{s}_K\,\Delta \ln\!\left(\frac{K}{H}\right)}_{\text{capital deepening}} + \underbrace{\Delta \ln A}_{\text{MFP}},

where YY is output, HH hours worked, KK capital services, s‾K\bar{s}_K the average capital income share, and AA the residual, multifactor productivity. The OECD series measures labour input in raw hours, so its MFP term includes any improvement in labour quality. The US Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) and the ABS’s quality-adjusted series separate out a labour-composition term (Labor Statistics, 2026; Statistics, 2026b). This distinction matters when US and Australian figures are compared (section 4).

The differences have six sources (P. Commission, 2026a; Statistics, n.d.):

  • Coverage. The ABS publishes MFP for the market sector only. The OECD covers the whole economy, including health, education and public administration, where output is largely measured by inputs.
  • Output concept. The OECD uses GDP; the ABS uses gross value added.
  • Timing. Financial-year windows that begin in 2019–20 capture the COVID-era composition spike in productivity. A calendar-2019 base largely does not.
  • Start-year sensitivity. In the PC’s quarterly series, whole-economy growth is about 0.15% a year from June 2019 but about 0.02% from December 2019.
  • Labour-input basis. Market-sector MFP in 2024–25 was −0.8% on a quality-adjusted labour basis and −0.5% on an hours basis.
  • Arithmetic and vintage. The PC uses percentage changes and the ABS log changes; for example, mining MFP is −3.2% in PC figures and −3.3% in ABS figures. The 2025 annual national accounts also revised hours worked.

The ILO’s modelled series puts Australian growth well above the OECD figure. But its Australia–US gap, about 1.5 percentage points a year, is close to the OECD’s 1.8. We therefore treat the size of the gap as robust and Australia’s own growth rate as uncertain within a band of roughly 0–0.5% a year.

Level comparisons depend on the PPP vintage. The ICP 2021 revisions moved Australia, Canada and the UK “further from top OECD performers” (OECD, 2024), and earlier Treasury work put Australia at 75–85% of US GDP per hour over four decades ((n, n.d.). The fall from 95% to 81% should therefore be read as a fall within the current vintage.

3 Stylised facts

Fact 1: zero hourly growth since 2019, after two decades of slowing. Australian hourly productivity growth fell from 1.33% a year in 2005–14 to 0.74% in 2014–19, and then to 0.10% (Figure 1). The quarterly index spiked in 2020–21, when low-productivity hours dropped out of the denominator, and then returned to its trend. The market-sector index stands 7.5% above its June 2014 level. The non-market index stands 2.5% below it, and is now lower than in March 2007 (P. Commission, 2026c).

Fact 2: the gap with the US widened, and most comparators fell with Australia. Measured against the United States, every economy in Figure 2 lost ground after 2019. Australia fell from 89% to 81% of the US level, Germany from 107% to 97%, the UK from 93% to 85% and Canada from 76% to 70%.

Fact 3: Australia’s slowdown after 2019 was among the deepest. Of fourteen OECD economies, only France and Italy grew more slowly than Australia over 2019–25. Korea, the United States and New Zealand accelerated (Figure 3).

Fact 4: living standards rose because hours rose. Australian GDP per capita grew 0.97% a year over 2019–25. Over the same period hours worked per head of population rose 5.3%, or about 0.87% a year. Almost all of the per-capita gain therefore came from working more hours per person (Figure 5). France and Italy followed the same job-rich, productivity-poor path. The United States raised GDP per capita by 1.81% a year with slightly fewer hours per head.

Fact 5: Southeast Asia is split. On ILO estimates, Singapore produces about 140% of Australia’s output per hour and has grown 2.1% a year since 2019 (Figure 7). Vietnam (4.3%) and Indonesia (2.6%) are catching up from low levels. Thailand (0.4%) and resource-dependent Brunei (0.1%) have stalled.

Three conclusions follow.

Australia’s MFP has been weak for twenty years, not five. On ABS data, market-sector MFP averaged 1.0% a year over 1998–99 to 2003–04, −0.2% over 2003–04 to 2009–10, and 0.7% over 2009–10 to 2017–18 (Statistics, 2026b). The PC reports a 20-year average of 0.4% a year, against 1.6% over 1994–95 to 2003–04 (P. Commission, 2026a). MFP fell in nine of sixteen industries in 2024–25. Composition adjustment makes the post-2019 picture worse, not better. The RBA’s real-time labour-quality index attributes about two-thirds of market-sector labour productivity growth over 2018–19 to 2023–24 to rising education and experience, so quality-adjusted growth was about 0.3% a year (Bruno et al., 2025).

Capital deepening has stalled. The PC attributes the 1.1% rise in market-sector labour productivity in 2023–24 “almost entirely” to capital deepening (P. Commission, 2025a). Over longer windows, capital per worker has been “broadly unchanged” since well before the pandemic (Plumb, 2025). Christensen reports that market-sector capital productivity fell 18.6% between 1995 and 2025, and that market-sector R&D as a share of gross fixed capital formation fell by more than 40% (Christensen, 2026). On OECD 2020–24 averages, about two-thirds of the Australia–US gap is MFP and one-third capital deepening.

The United States is the outlier. BLS private-business TFP grew 1.0% a year over 2019–25, against 0.6% over 2007–19, and capital intensity grew 2.3–2.7% a year in 2024–25 (Labor Statistics, 2026). The OECD’s larger US MFP figure (+1.34 points) differs because it covers the whole economy, uses a different window, and does not separate out labour composition. The other comparators look like Australia. Canadian business MFP averaged about −0.1% a year over 2019–22 (S. Canada, 2024). UK market-sector MFP was 0.7% below its 2019 level in 2024 (National Statistics, 2025).

4.1 Is it mismeasurement?

Leading tests of the mismeasurement hypothesis reject it as an explanation of the US slowdown. Correcting IT hardware prices makes that slowdown larger, and across countries the size of the slowdown is unrelated to ICT intensity (Byrne et al., 2016; Syverson, 2017). The Australian evidence is consistent with this. Information media and telecommunications (+4.1%) and finance (+3.1%) were among the strongest MFP performers in 2024–25 (Statistics, 2026b). Australia’s material measurement problems lie elsewhere:

  • Non-market output. The PC’s quality-adjusted measure of healthcare productivity grows about 3% a year (P. Commission, 2024a).
  • Labour quality. The ABS’s labour-quality index interpolates between Censuses.
  • Mining and electricity output. Measured output omits depletion, reliability and emissions (section 6).
  • Hours and PPPs. Revisions to hours worked and changes of PPP vintage move the figures.

Each of these corrections would raise measured growth in exactly the sectors where policy has expanded activity.

5 Hours, migration and the care economy

5.1 A surge in employment, not longer hours

Between March 2020 and March 2025, Australian hours worked rose 12.8% and employment 13.2%, so hours per worker were roughly flat (Statistics, 2025). The PC describes “a record surge in hours worked” between September 2021 and June 2023. It attributes the surge to pent-up demand, record labour-force participation and migrant inflows, and judges migrant supply “broadly as expected” (P. Commission, 2025e). Canada’s population grew 3.2% in 2023, but employment did not keep pace, so its hours per head fell (McCormack & Wang, 2024). In the euro area, employment rose while average hours per worker fell, so output per worker declined while output per hour rose (E. C. Bank, 2024). International comparisons must therefore be made per hour, not per worker.

5.2 COVID composition effects washed out

In the PC’s shift-share analysis, about 86% of the change in productivity between December 2019 and December 2020 came from employment moving between industries. Only 9% of the 6.1% fall between March 2022 and June 2023 did (P. Commission, 2025e). BLS, Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco and ONS studies document the same mechanism (National Statistics, 2021; San Francisco, 2024; Stewart, 2022). For a comparison of 2019 with 2025, these effects broadly net out. The PC and the RBA disagree about the 2022–23 fall. The PC emphasises inexperienced new entrants. The RBA’s direct labour-quality measure attributes only about 0.4 points of a 3.5% fall to lower labour quality (Bruno et al., 2025). We give more weight to the more direct measure.

5.3 Migration: temporary capital dilution, better-evidenced misallocation

Official analyses confirm a temporary capital-shallowing effect: capital growth has not kept pace with the growth in hours worked. In Australia this reflects “long term investment weakness … rather than a COVID-19-specific phenomenon” (P. Commission, 2025e). The Bank of Canada modelled negative capital deepening in 2024 as population growth outpaced capital accumulation (B. of Canada, 2024). In both countries, investment per worker was weak before the migration surge. Claims that migration is the primary cause of the stagnation come from advocacy sources and are not supported by official analysis (MacroBusiness, 2025).

The better-evidenced channel is visa composition. Matched employer–employee data show skilled-visa holders concentrated in more productive firms, and student and working-holiday visa holders in less productive firms (e61 Institute, 2023). This is misallocation, not dilution, and should be modelled separately. Treasury’s Intergenerational Report migration scenarios explicitly exclude productivity effects (Treasury, 2021).

5.4 The care economy

The non-market sector accounted for about 55% of filled-jobs growth and 52.6% of hours growth between March 2020 and March 2025. Health care and social assistance supplied about 70% of that, with hours up 39% (Statistics, 2025). The RBA estimates that the rising non-market share lowered economy-wide labour productivity growth by about 0.3 percentage points a year over 2017–18 to 2023–24, double the drag of the previous decade (Plumb, 2025). The PC traces the expansion to the National Disability Insurance Scheme (NDIS), new aged-care standards and childcare subsidies. It warns that if quality is mismeasured, “the post-pandemic decline in measured productivity is exaggerated” (P. Commission, 2025e).

France offers a close European parallel. The Banque de France attributes 1.2 points of France’s 8.5% productivity shortfall to subsidised apprenticeships and 1.7 points to labour hoarding (Devulder et al., 2024). Both countries expanded employment by policy in activities with low measured productivity. Unlike the euro area (TBC, n.d.), however, Australia’s labour market shows strong demand rather than hoarding.

6 Mining, electricity and construction

Cumulative MFP growth by industry over 2001–02 to 2023–24 ranges from +62% in information technology to −35% in utilities (Figure 6). The three industries with the largest declines fell for different reasons.

6.1 Mining: depletion and investment lags, then weather and lower ore grades

Mining MFP fell 3.3% in 2024–25, its fifth consecutive decline, which the ABS attributes to weather disruptions (Statistics, 2026b). Cumulatively it fell 34% between 2001–02 and 2023–24. Topp et al. showed that the 24% fall over 2000–01 to 2006–07 reflected declining ore yields and the lag between investment and production. Once those effects are removed, other factors raised mining MFP by about 8% (Topp et al., 2008). The ABS’s experimental series that counts mineral and energy resources as capital substantially reduces the measured decline (Statistics, 2014). Canada’s oil sands show the same mechanism: when high prices make costly reserves profitable to extract, measured productivity falls (Sharpe & Bradley, 2009). Norway reports productivity for its “mainland” economy separately from petroleum, a convention Australia could follow by publishing MFP excluding mining and utilities (N. P. Commission, 2015).

6.2 Electricity: an investment phase that MFP records as decline

In the PC’s experimental estimate, the electricity capital stock rose 126% while output rose 14%. Each dollar of output now requires about $10 of capital, against just over $2 for the economy as a whole. About 37% of investment since 2017–18 has gone to renewables and storage, whose capacity factors are lower than those of coal generation (P. Commission, 2026b). Adjusting for reliability using Australian Energy Regulator (AER) benchmarking reduces the decline in distribution productivity from 24% to 8%, and in transmission from 26% to 15%. Valuing the 30% fall in emissions at $67 a tonne turns a 32% fall in electricity productivity into a 23% fall. The PC compares this period to “the ‘investment phase’ of a mining boom”. Germany shows the same symptom, falling measured productivity, from a different cause. The IMF attributes about 60% of Germany’s downturn to lower potential growth, with the 2022 energy-price shock amplifying it (Fund, 2023; Kemp, 2026). Germany suffered a price shock as an energy importer. Australia is going through a capital-intensive transition as an energy exporter.

6.3 Construction: dwellings per hour have halved

Dwellings completed per hour worked fell 53% over about thirty years, while economy-wide labour productivity rose 49% (P. Commission, 2025d). Productivity fell in detached-house building and rose in higher-density building, which points to regulation and site constraints rather than technology. Some critics argue that physical measures understate quality improvements (Murray, n.d.). Because generation and transmission construction is classified as construction output, the energy transition competes with housing for the same workers (P. Commission, 2026b).

7 Investment, competition, dynamism and diffusion

7.1 Investment and intangibles

Non-mining business investment has fallen 3.2 percentage points as a share of GDP since the GFC, and capital deepening, historically Australia’s main source of productivity growth, “has stalled” (A. Robson & Sterland, 2025). The gap with the United States is probably widest in intangible investment, such as software, R&D and organisational capital. US intangible investment reached 15.6% of GDP in 2025, more than tangible investment (WIPO & Luiss, 2026). The latest Australian estimate is for 2005–06 (Barnes & McClure, 2009), which is itself a gap in the evidence. Canada is the cautionary comparator. Its business investment per worker fell from 87% to 54% of the US level between 2014 and 2024 (F. Institute, 2026, advocacy-leaning; see also W. B. P. Robson & Bafale, 2025).

7.2 Competition and markups

Australian firm-level data (from the ABS business-longitudinal dataset, BLADE) show the top-four firm share of industry sales rising about 2 points, to 43%, between 2001–02 and 2018–19. Average markups rose about 5–6%, and more than twice as fast in digitally intensive sectors (Day et al., 2022; Hambur, 2023). Weaker reallocation of activity between firms reduced annual labour productivity growth by about 0.1 points, roughly a fifth of the slowdown since 2012. Calibrating an Edmond–Midrigan–Xu model, Hambur and Freestone estimate that productivity and GDP would be 1–3% higher had competition stayed at mid-2000s levels (Hambur & Freestone, 2025). King (King, 2023) argues that the increases in concentration “are not huge”. We read competition as a material but not dominant explanation.

7.3 Diffusion to laggards

Across 23 countries, global frontier firms kept accelerating in the 2000s while laggards stalled (Andrews et al., 2016). Australia fits this pattern closely. Between 2002 and 2016, labour productivity rose 60% at the global frontier, 25% at Australian frontier firms and 15% at Australian laggards. The speed at which laggards caught up fell about 9%, and declining dynamism and competition explain one-half to three-quarters of that slowdown (Andrews et al., 2022). The firm entry rate fell from 14.5% to 11.5% over the same period, and job switching fell from 11.3% to 8.5% by 2019 (e61 Institute, 2025). In the United States, by contrast, business formation surged after 2020, shifting activity toward young firms (Decker & Haltiwanger, 2023). The PC argues that Australia’s JobKeeper wage subsidy limited the equivalent reallocation (P. Commission, 2025e).

7.4 AI and technology adoption

Australian business use of AI rose from 1% in 2022–23 to 12% in 2024–25 (Statistics, 2026a). In the United States, faster productivity growth is concentrated in industries with high AI adoption, but AI explains little of the aggregate acceleration so far (Çakır Melek & Miller, 2026). Micro-level gains are large: generative AI raised customer-support productivity by 14%, and by 34% for novices (Brynjolfsson, n.d.). Macro estimates are modest, with a TFP gain of at most about 0.7% over ten years (Acemoglu, 2025). The productivity J-curve (Brynjolfsson et al., 2021) predicts that measured TFP falls while firms build the complementary intangible capital that new technology requires. Australia’s weak intangible investment is therefore likely to be the binding constraint on gains from AI.

The comparison changes what needs explaining. Weak MFP and stalled capital deepening are common across the advanced economies; the United States is the exception. The forces behind the US acceleration were largely absent from every comparator, not only Australia: deep capital and intangible investment, reallocation into young firms, and early diffusion of digital technology (Decker & Haltiwanger, 2023; Labor Statistics, 2026). Australia’s distinctive features are the additional drags set out in sections 5–7.

In Southeast Asia, Singapore sets a 2–3% annual productivity target and subsidises firms’ access to AI and computing (D. I. Singapore, 2025; G. of Singapore, 2026). The World Bank reports that Thailand’s structural transformation has stalled (W. Bank, 2025). Brunei shows how quickly hydrocarbon revenue can erode (Fund, 2024). Indonesia’s export bans to force downstream nickel processing raised domestic value added but drew IMF criticism for lacking cost–benefit analysis (Zhejiang University, 2025). That is relevant to Australia’s own green-metals ambitions.

9 Policy implications

1. Improve measurement first. Australia’s statistical agencies should routinely publish productivity for the market sector excluding mining, quality-adjusted output for non-market sectors (following the UK’s post-Atkinson approach), and mining MFP that accounts for natural resources. This would sharpen accountability without excusing weakness in the market sector (P. Commission, 2024a; Statistics, 2014).

2. Lower the tax cost of investment. International practice is moving toward cash-flow treatment of investment, in which it is deducted immediately. The United States made 100% bonus depreciation permanent in 2025. In September 2026 Canada proposed a Productivity Mega Deduction, which the government claims would lower its marginal effective tax rate on new investment from 13.0% to 6.4% (Finance Canada, 2026). The PC proposed a company tax rate of 20% for firms under A$1b plus a 5% net cash-flow tax. This has not been legislated (P. Commission, 2025b). Without reform, Australia’s position relative to other countries worsens by default.

3. Revive competition policy and paid reform. The PC’s 2005 review credited the 1990s National Competition Policy with raising GDP by about 2.5% (P. Commission, 2005). A key design feature was that states were paid for reforms once these were independently assessed. The 2024 revival restores this model through a $900m National Productivity Fund, with modelled gains of 1.0–1.7% of GDP (Chalmers, 2024; P. Commission, 2024b). Today the gains lie in services, care, planning and occupational licensing.

4. Shorten the energy-transition investment phase. The PC estimates that accelerating approvals for wind generation and transmission by one year would cut electricity bills by about 7% over a decade (P. Commission, 2026b).

5. Reform housing and planning. Tilting the dwelling mix toward medium density would raise measured construction productivity (P. Commission, 2025d).

6. Manage the composition of migration. Visa composition and the use of migrants’ skills matter more than headline migration numbers (e61 Institute, 2023).

7. Focus AI policy on diffusion. Policy should support diffusion to laggard firms and investment in complementary intangible capital, rather than the technology frontier (P. Commission, 2025c).

8. Implement recommendations already made. The PC’s 2023 five-year inquiry made 71 recommendations, and reporting suggests few have been acted on (P. Commission, 2023; News, 2025). The five December 2025 reports made 47 more. The government says it is “progressing 13 of the 17 reform areas” (P. Commission, 2026c). The EU’s Draghi report illustrates the risk: only about 15% of its 383 recommendations had been fully implemented by January 2026 (Eunews, 2025). The literature on new industrial policy favours support that is time-limited and disciplined by export performance (Juhász et al., 2024). That is relevant to Australia’s Future Made in Australia agenda.

10 A research agenda

Seven questions are open and can be answered with Australian data.

  1. A formal bridge between OECD and ABS measures. No published source decomposes the gap between OECD whole-economy MFP and ABS market-sector MFP into coverage, output concept, timing, labour-input basis and data vintage. Section 2 sketches such a bridge; a full version is the natural next step.
  2. Quality-adjusted non-market output. Applying outcome-based measures of output (QALYs in health, value added in education) to ABS non-market output, benchmarked against the ONS, would test whether the drag of about 0.3 points a year is economic or statistical.
  3. Migration and capital per hour. Linking migrant employment shares to firm capital stocks in BLADE would separate capital dilution from misallocation by visa type.
  4. An AI J-curve in firm data. Event studies of firm TFP after AI and intangible adoption, using the Business Characteristics Survey linked to BLADE, would test the lags assumed in the PC’s A$116b scenario.
  5. Decomposing mining and electricity MFP. Updating Topp et al. (2008) and applying natural-capital-adjusted MFP (Topp et al., 2008) across Australia, Canada and Norway would separate depletion, weather, investment lags and unmeasured quality.
  6. Reallocation regimes after COVID. Comparing OECD firm-dynamics (DynEmp) entry series and job-to-job flows would test whether Australia’s job-retention approach, compared with the US quits surge, explains part of the divergence.
  7. A tracker of PC recommendations. Coding every PC recommendation since 2005 by whether and when it was implemented, modelled on the EPIC tracker of Draghi’s recommendations, would turn the political economy of reform into data. It would also allow ex-ante modelling (such as the 2024 National Competition Policy estimate) to be compared with ex-post outcomes.

Further gaps include the effect of ICP 2017 versus ICP 2021 PPPs on the Australia–US level gap, the difference between Labour Force Survey and Labour Account hours over 2019–25, harmonised AI-adoption rates across countries, and an up-to-date Australian marginal effective tax rate on investment.

11 Conclusion

Australia’s post-2019 stagnation is the point at which several slow-moving problems stopped being hidden. In turn, the mining boom, the terms of trade, and then rapid growth in population and hours kept income per person rising while output per hour lost momentum. Once the growth in hours is netted out, very little is left. The comparison with Canada and Europe matters as much as the comparison with the United States, because it shows that the forces behind the US acceleration were absent almost everywhere else. What is distinctively Australian is the combination of additional drags: a care economy, measured by its inputs, that has expanded by policy design; resource and electricity sectors in capital-heavy phases; and two decades of weaker firm entry and slower diffusion.

The question to ask is therefore not why Australian productivity collapsed, since measured MFP has been weak since the mid-2000s. It is why Australia’s growth model drifted from capital deepening and diffusion toward expanding labour input, and whether its statistics can distinguish real stagnation from statistical convention. The defensible headline is a range: roughly zero for the whole economy on OECD data, about 0.2–0.5% a year for the market sector, and about 1.0% for the market sector excluding mining. Every version is well below US growth and below Australia’s own history.

Appendix A. Data and replication

All data, code and figures are available at https://github.com/chid/australian-productivity. The script build/fetch_sources.sh downloads the raw source files, build/build_data.py produces the tidy CSVs in site/data/, and paper/figures/make_figures.py regenerates every figure. OECD series are whole-economy, calendar-year data; growth rates are compound annual rates between endpoint years; growth-accounting contributions are simple averages of annual percentage-point contributions. ILO data are used only for comparisons within the ILO series. PC quarterly data are seasonally adjusted, and their quarters are labelled by the month in which each quarter ends.
