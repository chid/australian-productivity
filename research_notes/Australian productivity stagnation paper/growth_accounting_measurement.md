# Growth accounting and measurement evidence on Australia's post-2019 labour productivity stagnation, in international comparison

Compiled 7 November 2026. Source types: [P] = primary official statistics or official agency publication; [PR] = peer-reviewed; [WP] = working/discussion paper (central bank, treasury, NBER); [TT] = think tank, industry or advocacy body; [M] = media/trade press. Figures labelled "derived" are my own arithmetic from published figures. They are not published numbers and must be checked before they are cited.

## Q1. What do the ABS, PC, RBA and Treasury say about MFP versus capital deepening in Australia since about 2004, and especially 2019-2025?

### Takeaway
On the ABS market sector, hours-worked basis, labour productivity fell 0.2% in 2024-25 and MFP fell 0.5% [P]. Annual MFP since 2019-20 has been volatile: −0.1, 0.7, 2.4, −0.9, 0.1, −0.5. The 20-year MFP average is only 0.3-0.4%/yr, against 1.6%/yr in 1994-95 to 2003-04 [P]. The PC put the 2023-24 rebound "almost entirely" down to capital deepening [P]. The RBA estimates that rising labour quality accounted for about two-thirds of market sector labour productivity growth from 2018/19 to 2023/24, which leaves a very small quality-adjusted residual [WP/P]. The RBA cut its trend productivity assumption from 1.0% to 0.7% in August 2025 [P].

### Inferences
- **Derived:** capital deepening's contribution to market sector labour productivity in 2024-25 is about +0.3 ppt on the hours basis (LP −0.2 minus MFP −0.5). This sits uneasily with the OECD whole-economy capital-deepening contribution for 2020-24 (−0.11 pp). The OECD figure covers the whole economy including the non-market sector, uses calendar years, and is GDP-based. Treat the gap as a scope difference, not necessarily a contradiction.
- **Derived from the ABS annual table (sum of log changes, 2020-21 to 2024-25, five years):**
  - LP ≈ +1.1 cumulative (≈0.22%/yr); MFP ≈ +1.8 cumulative (≈0.36%/yr).
  - This implies a slightly negative average capital-deepening contribution, about −0.14 ppt/yr, which is directionally consistent with the OECD −0.11 pp.
  - Including 2019-20 (six years), LP averages ≈0.45%/yr and MFP ≈0.28%/yr, so implied capital deepening is ≈+0.17 ppt/yr.
  - The sign of capital deepening for "since 2019" therefore depends on the base year, because the COVID hours collapse inflated LP in 2019-20 and 2020-21.
  - **Contradiction to flag:** ABS market sector MFP averages positive (≈+0.3%/yr) over 2019-20 to 2024-25, whereas the OECD MFP contribution for 2020-24 is −0.12 pp. The likely causes are coverage (whole economy including input-measured non-market industries), the calendar versus financial year, and vintage.
- **Derived from the 2017-18 to 2021-22 cycle:** LP ≈ GVA − hours ≈ 1.5%/yr and MFP 0.8, so capital deepening was ≈0.7 ppt/yr. This assumes the published capital and hours figures are growth rates, not contributions.
- **Labour quality:** the RBA's labour-quality result implies that on a QALI basis, the "true" MFP or quality-adjusted LP over 2018/19 to 2023/24 is near zero. Composition, not MFP, did much of the work, so the post-2019 problem is worse than hours-based figures suggest.
- **Since 2004:** the ABS cycle data show that MFP was negative in 2003-04 to 2009-10 (−0.2), driven by the mining investment boom and drought. It recovered in 2009-10 to 2017-18 (0.7%/yr). The weak 20-year average (0.3-0.4) is therefore not only a post-2019 phenomenon.

## Q2. How do the US BLS, Statistics Canada, ONS and EU KLEMS/INTANProd decompositions compare for the same period?

### Takeaway
The US is the outlier. BLS private nonfarm business TFP averaged 1.0%/yr over 2019-25, against 0.6% over 2007-19, with sizeable capital intensity and labour composition contributions [P]. Canada (MFP about −0.1%/yr 2019-22, −1.7% in 2023, negative capital intensity three years running) and the UK (MFP −0.6% in 2024, below its 2019 level) look more like Australia [P]. EU KLEMS/INTANProd only runs to 2021, so it gives little post-2019 evidence for Germany and France.

### Inferences
- **BLS versus the OECD context figure:** BLS TFP of 1.0%/yr (2019-25) is lower than the OECD-context figure of a +1.34 pp MFP contribution to US LP growth (2020-24). That is plausible given the different coverage (whole economy versus private nonfarm business), the window (2020-24 omits 2019 and 2025) and the treatment of labour composition. BLS TFP nets out labour composition; the OECD MFP measure usually does not. Note this when citing both.
- **Common pattern:** Canada and the UK share Australia's pattern of weak or negative MFP plus weak capital deepening. Australia is less an outlier versus Canada, the UK and Europe than versus the US. The striking US-Australia gap is a capital-intensity gap (US capital intensity +2.3 to 2.7%/yr) as much as a TFP gap.
- **Labour composition:** labour composition is positive in all countries (US 0.3-0.4 ppt, UK 0.7, Canada positive, Australia positive). So QALI-based MFP is below hours-based MFP everywhere, and cross-country comparisons must use a consistent basis.

- No official Destatis or INSEE growth-accounting figures for 2019-25 were found. EU KLEMS stops at 2021. For 2022-25 Germany and France, use the OECD Compendium 2026 or the Eurostat/ECB growth accounts (not retrieved).

## Q3. Measurement issues that may bias measured productivity

### Takeaway
The main measurement issues for Australia are:
- **Non-market output:** the non-market sector is largely input-measured, so measured productivity growth there is biased toward zero, and its rising share is a compositional drag. The PC's quality-adjusted health measure grows at about 3%/yr.
- **Labour-quality interpolation:** between Censuses, labour quality is interpolated.
- **Revisions:** national accounts and Labour Account hours were revised in 2025.
- **PPP vintages:** these affect cross-country level comparisons.
None of these plausibly reverses the post-2019 stagnation.

### Cited Findings
- **ABS method for non-market output:** individually consumed non-market services (e.g. education, hospitals) are measured by cost-weighted output volumes (FTE enrolments, hospital separations). Elsewhere, output is largely proxied by inputs. The ABS publishes MFP only for market sector industries, though it has experimental hospital MFP estimates. — [ABS Understanding productivity statistics](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/detailed-methodology-information/information-papers/understanding-abs-productivity-statistics); [ABS experimental hospital MFP](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/research/experimental-hospital-multifactor-productivity-estimates); [ABS Interpreting productivity statistics](https://www.abs.gov.au/articles/interpreting-abs-productivity-statistics); [ABS submission to PC inquiry 2022](https://assets.pc.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0011/347708/sub127-productivity.pdf)
- **PC health productivity:** PC (April 2024), *Advances in measuring healthcare productivity*, Research paper. Quality-adjusted productivity (using QALYs and health-adjusted life expectancy) grew by about 3%/yr across the subset of diseases studied, covering about one-third of the health sector. — [PC 2024](https://assets.pc.gov.au/research/completed/measuring-healthcare-productivity/measuring-healthcare-productivity.pdf); [APSC Research Note 03-25 (June 2025)](https://www.apsc.gov.au/sites/default/files/2025-11/RN%2003-25%20Measuring%20public%20service%20productivity%20a%20primer.pdf)
- **Critique of the non-market drag:** *Economic and Labour Relations Review* article "Is the public sector dragging down living standards?" [PR] argues that the non-market measurement convention biases measured productivity toward zero change. — [ELRR, Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/the-economic-and-labour-relations-review/article/is-the-public-sector-dragging-down-living-standards/999E04A996D00349F37F9166D904BF5B)
- **Labour quality interpolation:** the ABS QALI index interpolates linearly between Censuses (2016, 2021) and extrapolates after 2021. The RBA's LLFS-based index shows labour quality rising less over the pandemic than ABS QALI implies. — [Bruno, Hambur & Wang 2025](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/bulletin/2025/jul/a-closer-to-real-time-labour-quality-index.html)
- **Revisions and hours:** the 2024-25 MFP release includes the 2025 annual national accounts historical revisions and Labour Account hours methodology revisions. — [ABS 2026](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/industry/industry-overview/estimates-industry-multifactor-productivity/latest-release)
- **PPP vintages:** ICP 2021 results were published with revised 2017 PPPs. The OECD said Canada, Australia, the UK, Israel and Greece "moved further from top OECD performers" in 2021. — [OECD May 2024](https://www.oecd.org/en/data/insights/statistical-releases/2024/05/share-of-oecd-economies-in-global-gdp-broadly-stable-at-46-in-2021-compared-to-2017.html); [OECD PPP-ICP2021](https://search.oecd.org/sdd/prices-ppp/PPP-ICP2021-EN.pdf); [World Bank ICP 2021](https://www.worldbank.org/en/programs/icp/brief/ICP2021_DataViz)
  - Pre-ICP2021, Treasury put Australian GDP per hour at mostly 75-85% of the US over 40 years. — [Treasury, "Comparing productivity"](https://www.treasury.gov.au/sites/default/files/2019-03/04_comparing_productivity.pdf)
  - The PC 2026 bulletin's level comparison uses OECD PPP "2020 onwards" results and puts US non-agricultural market LP 23% above Australia in 2023. — [PC 2026 PDF](https://assets.pc.gov.au/2026-02/productivity-bulletin-2026.pdf)
- **OECD database quirk:** a DBnomics mirror of the OECD Productivity Levels dataset shows an apparent break in the Australian current-PPP GDP per hour series (2022 ≈78.4, 2023 ≈160.6). This suggests a units or vintage problem. Do not use it without checking. — [DBnomics OECD PDB_LV](https://db.nomics.world/OECD/DSD_PDB@DF_PDB_LV/AUS.A.GDPHRS._T.USD_PPP_H.V._Z._Z._Z)
- **Intangibles:** French and German differences in software and organisational capital measurement, around threefold, did not translate into LP divergence. Intangible capitalisation conventions affect measured capital deepening and TFP. — [VoxEU](https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/intangible-capital-france-and-germany-measurement-issues-and-their-impact)
  - In Australia, market sector R&D as a share of GFCF fell more than 40% between 1995 and 2025. — [PC 2026 PDF](https://assets.pc.gov.au/2026-02/productivity-bulletin-2026.pdf)
  - Bontadini et al. (2026) argue that missing intangibles bias TFP. — [CSLS IPM 46](https://csls.ca/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/IPM-Issue-46-1-Bontadini-Corrado-Haskel-Iommi-Jona-Lasinio-Miyagawa.pdf)
- **Mining price effects:** the PC 2025 bulletin appendix A.3 covers "Mining prices and productivity". Mining MFP is depressed by resource depletion and long investment lags, and mining is the largest negative direct contributor to 2024-25 market LP (−1.0 ppt). — [PC 2025](https://www.pc.gov.au/ongoing/productivity-insights/bulletins/bulletin-2025/); [ABS 2026](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/industry/industry-overview/estimates-industry-multifactor-productivity/latest-release)

### Inferences
- **Size of the non-market effect:**
  - The non-market convention depresses measured whole-economy (GDP/hour) productivity relative to the market sector.
  - This is a plausible reason why OECD whole-economy MFP for Australia (−0.12 pp, 2020-24) is weaker than ABS market sector MFP (≈+0.3%/yr derived) over the same years.
  - The non-market share has grown, with care economy and NDIS employment growth.
  - Quantifying the effect requires ABS non-market hours and GVA shares, which were not retrieved here.
- **Hours measurement:** the LFS and the national accounts/Labour Account differ in hours worked. The ABS productivity estimates use Labour Account hours. Recent methodological revisions mean the 2019-25 hours profile is vintage-dependent, so state the vintage (e.g. "ABS 5260.0.55.002, Feb 2026 release").
- **Multinational (Ireland-style) distortions** are minor for Australia. Its gap with Ireland is irrelevant, but EU27 aggregates include Ireland's 2015 "leprechaun" jump. This is not verified with a source here; see Gaps.

### Gaps
- No source was found that quantifies the LFS versus Labour Account hours gap for 2019-25.
- No source was found that gives the Australia-US GDP per hour gap under ICP 2017 versus ICP 2021 PPPs. Compute it from OECD PDB_LV vintages.
- No source was retrieved on Ireland and multinational profit-shifting distortions to EU aggregates (e.g. CSO modified GNI*). Look up CSO Ireland and Eurostat.
- No quantification was found of the non-market composition effect on Australian GDP per hour post-2019 (the e61 and PC quarterly bulletins may have it; not fetched).
- I found no ABS source on quality adjustment of ICT prices and hedonics in Australia post-2019.

## Q4. The mismeasurement-hypothesis literature (Syverson 2017; Byrne, Fernald & Reinsdorf 2016) and its relevance to Australia

### Takeaway
Both canonical papers conclude that mismeasurement of ICT and digital goods does not explain the post-2004 US slowdown [PR]. Syverson's cross-country test, which finds that slowdown size is unrelated to ICT intensity, transfers directly to Australia. Australia's distinctive measurement issues are different: non-market output, mining, labour quality, and hours/revisions, not free digital goods. Quiggin's earlier Australian critique concerns work intensity and cycle dating in the 1990s, not the post-2019 period.

### Cited Findings
- **Byrne, D.M., Fernald, J.G. & Reinsdorf, M.B. (2016)**, "Does the United States Have a Productivity Slowdown or a Measurement Problem?", *Brookings Papers on Economic Activity*, Spring 2016, 47(1): 109-182. — [Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/bpea-articles/does-the-united-states-have-a-productivity-slowdown-or-a-measurement-problem/); [PDF](https://www.brookings.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/03/byrnetextspring16bpea.pdf); [RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bin:bpeajo:v:47:y:2016:i:2016-01:p:109-182)
  - The slowdown is mainly real, with little evidence of growing mismeasurement of IT. Correcting IT hardware price mismeasurement makes the slowdown larger.
  - The slowdown is broad-based, not concentrated in hard-to-measure industries.
  - Free digital services are non-market consumer gains outside GDP.
  - One Brookings page lists Gordon as an author; the PDF lists only Byrne, Fernald and Reinsdorf. Cite the three.
- **Syverson, C. (2017)**, "Challenges to Mismeasurement Explanations for the US Productivity Slowdown", *Journal of Economic Perspectives* 31(2): 165-186 (NBER WP 21974, 2016). Four challenges:
  1. The slowdown occurred across dozens of countries, and its size is unrelated to ICT intensity.
  2. Surplus from digital goods falls well short of the missing output.
  3. Mismeasurement would require ICT-industry output to be multiples of what is measured.
  4. The GDI-GDP gap predates the slowdown and reflects capital income.
  - [NBER w21974](https://www.nber.org/papers/w21974); [RePEc JEP](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:aea:jecper:v:31:y:2017:i:2:p:165-86)
- **Quiggin, J.** argued that the mid-1990s Australian "productivity surge" was partly a statistical illusion, driven by unmeasured work intensity and arbitrary cycle peaks, and stressed asymmetric measurement error. — [Quiggin, RBA 2000 conference discussion](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/confs/2000/pdf/forsyth-disc.pdf); [Quiggin, "The Lost Golden Age of Productivity Growth?", ANU Press ch. 7](https://press-files.anu.edu.au/downloads/press/n12514/pdf/ch07.pdf); [UQ working paper WPP11_1](https://rsmg.group.uq.edu.au/files/1114/WPP11_1.pdf); [Quiggin blog 2012](https://johnquiggin.com/2012/05/17/declining-productivity-at-the-productivity-commission/) [blog, advocacy-adjacent]
- **Parliamentary context:** the House of Representatives Economics Committee productivity report (ch. 3) records the cycle-dating dispute. — [APH report ch. 3](https://www.aph.gov.au/parliamentary_business/committees/house_of_representatives_committees?url=economics%2Fproductivity%2Freport%2Fchapter+3.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Transfer to Australia:** the Byrne-Fernald-Reinsdorf and Syverson tests transfer well. Australia's slowdown is broad (MFP fell in 9 of 16 industries in 2024-25). The rebound industries in 2024-25 include ICT (information, media and telecommunications +4.1) and finance (+3.1), which is inconsistent with a story in which digital mismeasurement is hiding growth.
- **Quiggin's work-intensity argument runs the other way post-2019:** if work intensity fell (hybrid work, labour hoarding), measured hours would overstate effective labour input. This is speculative and has no source here.
- **Australian-specific mismeasurement:** health (PC +3%/yr quality-adjusted) and the labour-quality interpolation are larger and more relevant than digital-goods mismeasurement. Neither flips the conclusion.

### Gaps
- No Australian empirical application of the Syverson cross-country test was found.
- Quiggin's commentary specifically on the 2019-26 stagnation was not found.

## Q5. Key academic papers on Australia's productivity slowdown

### Takeaway
The RBA/Treasury/e61 firm-level programme (Andrews, Hambur and co-authors) attributes much of the slowdown to slower diffusion from the frontier, slower capital and labour reallocation, and declining competition and rising mark-ups [WP]. The PC's 2025 MFP-sources work points to weaker use of new ideas.

### Cited Findings
- **Andrews, D., Hambur, J., Hansell, D. & Wheeler, A. (2022)**, "Reaching for the Stars: Australian Firms and the Global Productivity Frontier", Treasury Working Paper 2022-01. Uses BLADE and Orbis data. The slowdown reflects slower diffusion to non-frontier mature firms, not slower frontier innovation; young firms catch up faster. — [Treasury PDF](https://treasury.gov.au/sites/default/files/2022-02/p2022-243535.pdf); [e61 slides](https://e61.in/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/Reaching-for-the-stars.pdf)
- **Hambur, J. & Andrews, D. (2023)**, "Doing Less, with Less: Capital Misallocation, Investment and the Productivity Slowdown in Australia", RBA Research Discussion Paper 2023-03. Capital reallocation toward more productive firms has slowed, input allocation has become less efficient, and the effect is worse in debt-dependent sectors. — [RBA RDP 2023-03](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/rdp/2023/2023-03/full.html); [PDF](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/rdp/2023/pdf/rdp2023-03.pdf)
- **Andrews, D. & Hansell, D. (2021):** slower labour reallocation explains about a quarter of the labour productivity slowdown over 2012-16. This is as cited in RDP 2023-03; the original paper was not fetched. — [RBA RDP 2023-03](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/rdp/2023/2023-03/full.html)
- **RBA RDP 2025-05**, "How Costly are Mark-ups in Australia? The Effect of Declining Competition on …" (authors not captured; likely Hambur and co-authors). Rising mark-ups are linked to slower reallocation. — [RBA RDP 2025-05](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/rdp/2025/pdf/rdp2025-05.pdf)
- **RBA RDP 2023-10**, "Adoption of Emerging Digital General-purpose Technologies: Determinants and Effects". — [RBA RDP 2023-10](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/rdp/2023/2023-10/full.html)
- **Andrews, Deutscher, Hambur & Hansell (2019)**, "Wage Growth in Australia: Lessons from Longitudinal Microdata", RBA Annual Conference 2019. — [RBA conf 2019](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/confs/2019/pdf/rba-conference-2019-andrews-deutscher-hambur-hansell.pdf)
- **Other papers cited by the RBA (2026)** but not fetched: Nguyen & Hambur (2023); Bowman, Hambur & Markovski (2024); Hambur & Freestone (2025). — [RBA 2026 submission](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/submissions/labour-market-and-inflation/productivity-in-australia-02-2026/)
- **Ashburner, L. & Wong, V. (2025)**, "Learning but not always doing", PC Annual Productivity Bulletin 2025 feature. — [PC 2025](https://www.pc.gov.au/ongoing/productivity-insights/bulletins/bulletin-2025/)
- **Christensen, J. (2026)**, "Beyond the accumulation of capital", PC Annual Productivity Bulletin 2026 feature. — [PC 2026 PDF](https://assets.pc.gov.au/2026-02/productivity-bulletin-2026.pdf)
- **UNSW BusinessThink**, "A primer on Australian productivity" [M/university outreach]. — [UNSW](https://businessthink.unsw.edu.au/articles/australia-productivity-growth-slowdown-policy)

### Inferences
- **Consistency with the growth accounting:** the firm-level literature explains why MFP is weak, through diffusion and reallocation. Christensen's capital-productivity analysis, together with weak investment (PC September 2025), explains why capital deepening is weak. Both channels predate 2019. The post-2019 period overlays COVID composition effects, a mining MFP decline and non-market expansion.

### Gaps
- No e61 Institute paper specifically on the post-2019 slowdown was fetched. e61 is an independent research institute: label it [TT/independent research] when citing.
- Dolman's work (e.g. ANU/PC publications on productivity measurement and the "Dolman critique") was not located in this pass.
- The PC (2023), *5-year Productivity Inquiry: Advancing Prosperity*, was not fetched.
