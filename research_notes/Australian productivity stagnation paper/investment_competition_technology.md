*Research notes compiled 7 November 2026. Primary or official sources are used where they could be retrieved. Secondary, law-firm, media and advocacy sources are labelled. Bibliographic details for BibTeX are given inline, or in the "BibTeX-ready references" list at the end of the last section.*

## Q1. Australian non-mining business investment, capital per hour and intangible investment vs US/Canada/Europe

### Takeaway
Official Australian sources (PC, Treasury) now attribute much of Australia's post-mid-2010s productivity stagnation to stalled capital deepening. The PC puts the fall in non-mining investment at 3.2 percentage points of GDP since the GFC. I could not retrieve primary series for 2000–2026 investment shares, capital per hour or a current INTANProd Australia–US intangibles comparison. Those remain gaps, to be filled from ABS 5206/5204, OECD and INTANProd downloads.

### Cited Findings
- PC commissioners Alex Robson and Barry Sterland (AFR op-ed reproduced on the PC site, 1 Aug 2025) state that **non-mining investment has fallen by 3.2 percentage points as a share of GDP since the Global Financial Crisis**. They say capital deepening historically drove Australian productivity growth and "has stalled". — [Robson & Sterland, "Reform company tax to spark growth", Productivity Commission, 2025](https://www.pc.gov.au/media-speeches/articles/reform-company-tax)
- In its final report, *Creating a more dynamic and resilient economy* (released 19 Dec 2025), the PC finds that business investment has "fallen notably" over the past decade, contributing to lacklustre productivity. Secondary coverage reports the PC as attributing stagnation to a lack of capital deepening since the mid-2010s. — [PC, Five pillars of productivity inquiries](https://www.pc.gov.au/inquiries-and-research/five-productivity-inquiries/meeting-productivity-challenge); [Investor Daily (media)](https://www.investordaily.com.au/corporate-tax-reform-could-unlock-7-4bn-in-investment-for-australia-pc-report-reveals)
- Former Treasury Secretary Ken Henry argues that Australian productivity gains historically depended on capital per worker. He says the 21st century has seen "capital-shallowing" as non-mining physical investment declined, and that resource super-profits were not recycled into non-mining investment. — [ABC News, 17 Aug 2025 (media/opinion)](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2025-08-17/what-is-productivity-albanese-economic-roundtable/105655214)
- AICD commentary (secondary): non-mining business investment fell to about 7% of GDP by late 2012 and recovered only to about 9% by March 2025. An earlier AICD article put non-mining capex at 8.8% of GDP in 2017–18. Grattan described non-mining investment as "close to 50-year lows" as a share of GDP (2017). — [AICD, "What is fuelling Australia's productivity crisis"](https://www.aicd.com.au/economic-news/australian/trends/what-is-fuelling-australias-productivity-crisis.html); [AICD, public investment](https://www.aicd.com.au/economic-news/australian/infrastructure-projects/public-investment-could-save-the-australian-economy.html); [Grattan Institute, *Reigniting investment* (2017)](https://grattan.edu.au/?p=4988)
- Ai Group (industry body): in Q1 2025, investment in the non-mining market sector fell 1.1% and equipment and machinery investment fell 2.5%. — [Ai Group, Key Australian industry indicators, June 2025](https://www.australianindustrygroup.com.au/resourcecentre/research-economics/economics-intelligence/2025/key-australian-industry-indicators-june-2025/)
- Recent capex strength (Q4 2025) was concentrated in renewables and data centres (market commentary). — [InvestingLive, 26 Feb 2026 (media)](https://investinglive.com/news/australia-q4-capex-beats-expectations-as-renewable-investment-lifts-outlook-20260226)
- RBA's earlier structural account: the December 2014 Bulletin documents that non-mining investment was subdued relative to earlier cycles, and the long-run decline in the non-mining investment share partly reflects a shift in industry structure towards less capital-intensive services. — [RBA Bulletin, Dec 2014, "Cycles in Non-mining Business Investment"](https://rba.gov.au/publications/bulletin/2014/dec/1.html)
- **Intangibles, US:** WIPO/Luiss *World Intangible Investment Highlights 2026* puts **US intangible investment at 15.6% of GDP in 2025, against 10.3% for tangible investment**. US intangible investment grew 5.6% a year over 2015–2025, about three times the tangible rate of 1.9%. — [WIPO, World Intangible Investment Highlights 2026](https://www.wipo.int/web-publications/world-intangible-investment-highlights-2026/en/key-trends-and-insights.html)
- **Intangibles, EU vs US (older):** EIB estimates US intangible investment averaged 4.2% of GDP over 2000–2013, against 3.1% in the EU-14, on a narrower asset definition. — [EIB Economics Working Paper 2016/08](https://www.eib.org/files/efs/economics_working_paper_2016_08_en.pdf)
- **Intangibles, Australia (dated):** a PC staff working paper (Barnes & McClure, 2009) estimates market-sector intangible investment at about $57b in 2005–06, almost half the size of tangible investment. Organisational capital was about 27% of intangibles and computerised information about 13%, and roughly 80% was not capitalised in the national accounts. Capitalising intangibles did not materially change Australia's measured MFP profile, unlike the US, where intangibles account for much of the mid-1990s acceleration. — [PC Staff Working Paper, *Investments in Intangible Assets and Australia's Productivity Growth*](https://www.pc.gov.au/inquiries-and-research/intangible-investment/)
- OECD work on intangibles finds that financing frictions (collateral constraints) limit intangible investment, and that intangible-intensive firms rely more on equity. — [Demmou & Franco, OECD ECO WP No. 1681 (2021), "Mind the financing gap"](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2021/09/mind-the-financing-gap-enhancing-the-contribution-of-intangible-assets-to-productivity_d2e72eb1/7aefd0d9-en.pdf)

### Inferences
- With US intangibles at about 15.6% of GDP, the Australia–US gap is probably larger in intangibles (software, R&D, organisational capital) than in tangible capex. That fits the frontier-diffusion and digital-markup evidence in Q2. This is an inference: no comparable Australian INTANProd figure was retrieved.
- Australian capital shallowing in 2022–25 likely reflects both weak non-mining investment and rapid hours growth driven by population. Commentators (MacroBusiness, advocacy) and reportedly RBA staff link this to immigration, but I did not verify RBA primary text.

### Gaps
- No primary ABS or RBA annual series for non-mining business investment/GDP over 2000–2026 was retrieved. Pull it from ABS 5206.0 (private GFCF by asset) and the RBA Chart Pack.
- Capital stock per hour worked (ABS 5260.0.55.002 Estimates of Industry Multifactor Productivity: capital deepening contribution): not retrieved.
- OECD INTANProd Australia vs US/Canada/EU shares: not retrieved, and the WIPO 2026 snippets did not include Australia.
- RBA Michael Plumb's March 2025 capital-shallowing remarks are reported only via MacroBusiness and are unverified.

## Q2. Competition and concentration: markups, concentration, merger reform, and frontier–laggard divergence

### Takeaway
A consistent body of Treasury and RBA firm-level work (BLADE) finds declining competitive pressure since the early 2000s. Four-firm concentration rose about 2 points, average markups rose about 5–6%, and incumbency increased. Allocative efficiency weakened, and Australian laggards converge to the global frontier more slowly. Calibrated GE estimates put the misallocation cost at 1–3% of GDP/TFP, and lower dynamism and competition explain half to three-quarters of the convergence slowdown. Mandatory, suspensory merger control began 1 January 2026.

### Cited Findings
**Markups and concentration (Treasury/RBA, primary)**
- Treasury Round Up (Oct 2022), "Competition in Australia and its impact on productivity growth" (Day, Duretto, Hartigan & Hambur), BLADE data:
  - **The top four firms in each 4-digit ANZSIC industry held about 43% of industry sales on average in 2018–19, about 2 percentage points higher than in 2001–02.** HHI shows a similar rise.
  - **Incumbency: about 75% of firms in an industry's top four in 2016–17 were still there in 2018–19, up from about 71% in 2001–02.**
  - **Average firm markups rose about 6% between 2003–04 and 2016–17.** This is a little smaller than De Loecker & Eeckhout's (2018) estimate for Australia. The rise is broad-based and driven by within-firm increases, not reallocation to high-markup firms, which the authors read as evidence against the "superstar firm" hypothesis for Australia.
  - **The markup increase was more than twice as large in the most digitally intensive sectors.**
  - In industries with rising markups, productive firms grow employment more slowly and unproductive firms are less likely to exit. **Reduced dynamic allocation lowered annual labour productivity growth by about 0.1 percentage points, roughly one-fifth of the observed slowdown since 2012** (Hambur 2021).
  — [Treasury Round Up, Oct 2022](https://treasury.gov.au/sites/default/files/2022-10/p2022-325290-productivity-growth.pdf)
- Hambur (2021), Treasury Working Paper, "Product market power and its implications for the Australian economy". It concludes that part of the markup rise reflects technological change, but that market power has also increased and competition declined. — [Treasury WP 2021](https://treasury.gov.au/publication/p2021-177591)
- The published version, Hambur (2023), "Product Market Competition and its Implications for the Australian Economy", *Economic Record* 99(324): 32–57, reports **markups rising by about 5% since the mid-2000s**, less than previously documented for Australia. The 6% (WP/Round Up) and 5% (journal) figures come from different vintages. — [EconPapers record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:ecorec:v:99:y:2023:i:324:p:32-57)
- Media reporting of the WP version: markups for the most digitally intensive firms rose 12%, against 4% for all other firms, over 2003–2016. — [The New Daily, Andrew Leigh opinion, Dec 2022](https://thenewdaily.com.au/opinion/2022/12/27/andrew-leigh-mark-ups/)
- **Hambur & Freestone (2025), RBA RDP 2025-05**, "How Costly are Mark-ups in Australia? The Effect of Declining Competition on Misallocation and Productivity". It calibrates the Edmond, Midrigan & Xu (2023) GE model to Australian microdata. Abstract: "If the degree of competition in the Australian economy had not declined from mid-2000s levels, how much higher would aggregate productivity and GDP be due to resources being better allocated across firms…? The answer, according to this model, is **1–3 per cent**." Other channels imply even larger costs, though these are less precisely estimated. Freestone is with Treasury's Competition Taskforce Division. — [RBA RDP 2025-05](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/rdp/2025/2025-05.html)
- Hambur & La Cava (RBA research note) find retail trade became more competitive recently, after declining competition through the 2000s. This is a sectoral counterpoint. — [EconBiz record](https://www.econbiz.de/Record/business-concentration-and-mark-ups-in-the-retail-trade-sector-hambur-jonathan/10012270355)
- Champion, Edmond & Hambur (2023), RBA Annual Conference, "Competition, Markups, and Inflation: Evidence from Australian Firm-level Data". In the benchmark calibration, variable markups dampen inflation pass-through. — [RBA conference presentation](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/confs/2023/pdf/rba-conference-2023-champion-edmond-hambur-presentation.pdf)
- Political and advocacy framing: Andrew Leigh (Assistant Minister for Competition) cites analysis that more than half of Australian markets are concentrated, with the top four holding over 80% in some large industries. MGA Independent Businesses Australia (advocacy) claims 10 of the 20 largest industry classes are highly concentrated, using a one-third four-firm threshold. — [Leigh, Sydney Institute address, 7 Dec 2022](https://ministers.treasury.gov.au/ministers/andrew-leigh-2022/speeches/address-sydney-institute-sydney); [MGA submission (advocacy)](https://www.accc.gov.au/system/files/MGA-Independent-Businesses-Australia-Treasury-Consultation-Paper_0.pdf)
- Dissent: Stephen King (Monash; former PC Commissioner) argues the rises in concentration identified by Treasury and e61 "are not huge", and that the competition role in falling dynamism is "far from clear". Job churn also falls when firms face fewer shocks. — [King, Monash Lens, Sep 2023](https://lens.monash.edu/yes-australian-businesses-have-become-less-dynamic-but-there-are-bigger-reasons-for-our-sliding-productivity-growth/). ECIPE (think tank) argues concentration and markup data are misleading guides for merger policy. — [ECIPE blog](https://ecipe.org/blog/navigating-merger-reforms-in-australia)

**Frontier vs laggard divergence**
- **Andrews, Criscuolo & Gal (2016), "The Best versus the Rest", OECD Productivity Working Paper No. 5.** Using OECD-Orbis data across 23 countries, global frontier firms (top 5% by labour productivity or MFP in each 2-digit industry) are larger, more profitable, younger and more likely to patent. Frontier productivity kept growing in the 2000s while laggards stagnated, so the gap reflects slowing diffusion. Diffusion was slower in markets with less pro-competitive product market regulation. — [Brookings/Hutchins WP 24 version](https://www.brookings.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/09/wp24_andrews-et-al_final.pdf); [OECD, Frontier firms, technology diffusion and public policy (2015)](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/frontier-firms-technology-diffusion-and-public-policy_5jrql2q2jj7b-en.html)
- **Andrews, Hambur, Hansell & Wheeler (2022), "Reaching for the Stars: Australian Firms and the Global Productivity Frontier", Treasury Working Paper 2022-01 (7 Feb 2022)**, merging OECD-Orbis with BLADE:
  - **Global frontier labour productivity was 60% higher in 2016 than in 2002, Australian frontier firms only 25% higher, and Australian laggards 15% higher.** The gap opens from about 2009 and is most pronounced in services.
  - **The convergence speed coefficient was 0.443 in 2002–04 and about 9% lower by 2011–16.** The slowdown was larger in services than in manufacturing.
  - **The average annual BLADE firm entry rate fell from 14.5% to 11.5% between 2004 and 2016.** Moving a low-entry industry (10th percentile, 8% entry) to a high-entry rate (90th percentile, 16%) raises convergence speed by 4.8 percentage points, about 10%.
  - Firms in higher entry, exit and turnover industries, and in lower-markup industries, converge faster. **Declines in dynamism and competitive pressure account for half to three-quarters of the slowdown in convergence.**
  — [Treasury WP 2022-01 (PDF)](https://treasury.gov.au/sites/default/files/2022-02/p2022-243535.pdf)

**Merger reform 2024–26**
- The *Treasury Laws Amendment (Mergers and Acquisitions Reform) Act 2024* introduced a mandatory, suspensory ACCC notification regime. It was voluntary from 1 July 2025 and **mandatory from 1 January 2026**, with some asset and voting-power thresholds from 1 April 2026 and annual indexation from 1 January 2027. Reported economy-wide thresholds: combined Australian revenue of at least A$200m and target revenue of at least A$10m, or acquirer group revenue of at least A$500m with other tests. A waiver fee of $8,300 is also reported. These details come from law-firm summaries and should be verified against the Notification of Acquisitions Determination. — [White & Case](https://www.whitecase.com/insight-alert/transition-mandatory-merger-control-australia-key-parameters-new-regime); [Maddocks](https://www.maddocks.com.au/australias-new-merger-notification-regime); [Bird & Bird 2026](https://cm.twobirds.com/en/insights/2026/australia/australia's-new-merger-regime-its-not-getting-any-easier!)
- Treasurer Chalmers framed the Competition Taskforce (announced Aug 2023) around evidence of rising concentration and falling dynamism. — [Treasurer media release, 23 Aug 2023](https://ministers.treasury.gov.au/node/63851)

### Inferences
- Both the within-firm channel (slower frontier catch-up; Andrews et al. 2022) and the between-firm channel (weaker reallocation; Hambur 2021, Hambur & Freestone 2025) point to competition as a quantitatively material but not dominant explanation. Reallocation accounts for about one-fifth of the post-2012 slowdown, misallocation for 1–3% of the GDP level, and dynamism and competition for half to three-quarters of the convergence slowdown.
- The twice-as-large markup rise in digital-intensive sectors links the competition story to technology diffusion (Q4). Digital scale economies plausibly raise both frontier productivity and market power.
- The merger reform is too recent (in force about 10 months) for outcome evidence.

### Gaps
- The Treasury Competition Taskforce has published no 2024–26 per-industry concentration table that I could find.
- No post-2019 BLADE update of markups or concentration was located.
- ACCC 2026 merger-notification statistics (numbers notified, Phase 2 rates) were not retrieved.

## Q3. Business dynamism: Australian entry/exit trends vs the US post-2020 business formation surge

### Takeaway
Employer-firm entry in Australia declined from about 13–14.5% in the mid-2000s to about 11–11.5% by the late 2010s (BLADE). More recent entry has been concentrated in non-employing businesses. The US, by contrast, saw a durable post-2020 surge in business applications and employer startups, which Decker & Haltiwanger (BPEA 2023) find was real and shifted activity toward young firms. Applications hit a reported record of about 5.67m in 2025 (needs Census verification).

### Cited Findings
- BLADE average annual firm entry rate fell from **14.5% (2004) to 11.5% (2016)**. — [Andrews et al., Treasury WP 2022-01](https://treasury.gov.au/sites/default/files/2022-02/p2022-243535.pdf)
- Treasury analysis of employing businesses: entry rate fell from **13% in 2005–06 to 11% in 2018–19** (secondary reporting of Treasury). — [SmartCompany](https://www.smartcompany.com.au/business-advice/competition/andrew-leigh-competition-small-business-economy/)
- Bakhtiari (2017/2018, Dept of Industry, Innovation and Science), using BLADE for 2002–2015: firm entry has declined since 2005, while young firms still supply a large share of job creation. — [DISER, *Entrepreneurship dynamics in Australia: lessons from microdata*](https://www.industry.gov.au/publications/entrepreneurship-dynamics-australia-lessons-microdata)
- e61 Institute, *The Young and the Restless*: firms aged five or under (excluding first-year firms) account for about 40% of job creation (another e61-linked piece gives 59%; the definitions likely differ). Surviving entrants' productivity catches up to incumbents by age five. These findings are secondary reports. — [Business News Australia](https://www.businessnewsaustralia.com/articles/e61-report-shows-startups-outsized-economic-contribution.html); [inkl](https://www.inkl.com/news/young-businesses-create-6-in-10-new-jobs-in-australia-far-more-than-established-firms)
- King (2023): recent increases in Australian entry are mainly non-employing (sole traders and contractors). — [Monash Lens](https://lens.monash.edu/yes-australian-businesses-have-become-less-dynamic-but-there-are-bigger-reasons-for-our-sliding-productivity-growth/)
- **Decker & Haltiwanger (2023), "Surging Business Formation in the Pandemic: Causes and Consequences?", *Brookings Papers on Economic Activity* 54(2), Fall 2023, pp. 249–316.** The surge in applications translated into a real rise in new employer businesses, concentrated in industries tied to pandemic shifts in work and lifestyle. Activity shifted from large mature firms toward young small firms, and the surge was more closely linked to voluntary quits than to layoffs. The authors say it is too early to call a durable reversal of the pre-pandemic decline. — [BPEA PDF](https://www.brookings.edu/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/Decker-Haltiwanger_16820-BPEA-FA23_WEB.pdf); [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bin:bpeajo:v:54:y:2023:i:2023-02:p:249-316); [Brookings summary](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/surging-business-formation-in-the-pandemic-causes-and-consequences)
- US Census BFS 2025: secondary sites report **5,671,836 total business applications in 2025 (an all-time high, above 2023) and 1,708,842 high-propensity applications (about 30%)**. These annual sums are unverified against Census. The December 2025 BFS release (14 Jan 2026) exists. — [Census BFS Dec 2025 release](https://www.census.gov/econ/bfs/pdf/historic/bfs_2025m12.pdf); [FRED high-propensity series](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/data/BAHBATOTALNSAUS); [FounderReports (secondary)](https://founderreports.com/how-many-businesses-are-started-each-year/)

### Inferences
- The contrast suggests the Australia–US divergence after 2020 is partly a dynamism story. US entry rebounded toward young high-propensity employers, while Australian entry gains were mostly non-employing. A direct comparable cross-country employer entry series (e.g. OECD DynEmp/SDBS) would be needed to confirm this.

### Gaps
- No ABS *Counts of Australian Businesses* (8165.0) entry and exit rates for 2020–2026 were retrieved. Pull from ABS directly.
- No e61 primary report PDF or date was retrieved.
- Annual Census BFS 2025 totals were not verified against the primary Census table.
- No Canada, Europe or Singapore entry-rate comparisons were retrieved.

## Q4. Technology and AI adoption: US evidence, Australian adoption, Singapore

### Takeaway
US TFP growth has run faster in 2019–25 (1.0% a year) than in 2007–19 (0.6% a year). Kansas City Fed industry analysis finds AI adoption correlates with productivity gains but does not yet explain the aggregate shift. Micro evidence shows large task-level gains (+14% in customer support), while Acemoglu's macro bound is modest (≤0.66% TFP over 10 years). Australian AI adoption by businesses jumped from 1% (2022–23) to 12% (2024–25) on ABS measures. The PC's December 2025 data and digital report favours "enable first, regulate carefully" and estimates about A$116b of extra GDP over a decade. Singapore is subsidising enterprise compute directly (S$150m ECI).

### Cited Findings
**US**
- BLS (USDL-26-0504, 19 Mar 2026): **private nonfarm business TFP rose 0.8% in 2025** (output +2.6%, combined inputs +1.7%). The 2024 figure was revised to 1.5%. **TFP grew 1.0% a year over 2019–25 against 0.6% a year over 2007–19.** Labour productivity rose 2.2% in 2025 after 3.0% in 2024. — [BLS Total Factor Productivity 2025 release](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/prod3.nr0.htm); [archived PDF](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/prod3_03192026.pdf); [FRED MFPNFBS](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/MFPNFBS)
- **Çakır Melek & Miller (2026), "A New U.S. Productivity Chapter? What Industry Data Say About AI", FRB Kansas City *Economic Bulletin*, February, pp. 1–4.** US labour productivity has been well above its pre-pandemic trend since late 2022, with gains concentrated in a few industries. Higher-AI-adoption industries show faster productivity growth, but AI adoption explains little of the shift in aggregate contributions, which the authors read as adoption "still spreading". — [RePEc/IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/fip/fedkeb/102427.html)
- AI capex and GDP: AI-related spending contributed about 0.73–0.97 percentage points to US real GDP growth in 2025 on gross measures. Minneapolis Fed (Waugh) estimates a net contribution of only about 0.14 percentage points after imports, against about 0.75 gross. These come from secondary aggregators and need primary verification. — [harici.com.tr (secondary)](https://harici.com.tr/en/ai-boom-creates-analytical-challenge-for-central-banks-as-surging-spending-meets-import-offsets/); [roic.ai (secondary)](https://www.roic.ai/news/fed-officials-split-on-ais-economic-impact-as-productivity-evidence-remains-elusive-02-17-2026)
- **Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond, "Generative AI at Work"**, NBER WP 31161 (2023), published 2025 (journal reported as *QJE*, unconfirmed in search). A staggered rollout of an AI assistant to 5,179 customer-support agents raised issues resolved per hour by **14% on average and about 34–35% for novice and low-skilled workers**, with minimal effect for the most experienced. It also improved customer sentiment and retention. — [NBER w31161 (EconPapers)](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:nbr:nberwo:31161); [arXiv 2304.11771](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.11771v1)
- **Acemoglu (2025), "The simple macroeconomics of AI", *Economic Policy* 40(121): 13–58** (NBER WP 32487, May 2024). Using task exposure and micro productivity estimates, he finds a **TFP gain of no more than about 0.66% over 10 years**. Adjusting for "hard-to-learn" tasks gives **under 0.53%** (the MIT page says 0.55%, but the NBER and journal versions say 0.53%). — [NBER w32487](https://www.nber.org/papers/w32487); [EconPapers journal record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:ecpoli:v:40:y:2025:i:121:p:13-58.)

**Australia**
- ABS *Characteristics of Australian Business 2024–25* (released 25 Jun 2026): **12% of businesses reported using AI, up from 1% in 2022–23.** The survey has been redesigned into a biennial Business Characteristics Survey (Innovation and Digital modules combined, about 7,000 businesses). — [ABS media release](https://www.abs.gov.au/media-centre/media-releases/business-adoption-artificial-intelligence-accelerates-2024-25); [ABS CAB 2024–25](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/industry/technology-and-innovation/characteristics-australian-business/2024-25). Industry surveys report much higher figures (NAB: 42% of SMEs). The definitions are not comparable. — [IT Brief/NAB](https://itbrief.com.au/story/nab-says-42-of-aussie-smes-use-ai-to-boost-productivity)
- **PC (2025), *Harnessing data and digital technology*, Inquiry Report No. 111** (dated 10 Dec 2025, released 19 Dec 2025). Its pillars are enabling AI's productivity potential, copyright and AI, rightsizing data access, outcomes-based privacy, and digital financial reporting. It recommends a gap-analysis, "enable first, regulate carefully" approach in which AI-specific regulation is a last resort. Its plausible central scenario is **about A$116b of additional GDP over the next decade**. It defers copyright changes (no TDM exception) for about three years while monitoring impacts. Details come from law-firm summaries; the PDF is linked. — [PC report PDF](https://assets.pc.gov.au/2025-12/data-digital_0.pdf); [King & Wood Mallesons summary](https://www.mallesons.com/au/en/insights/latest-thinking/productivity-commission-releases-final-report-into-harnessing-data-and-digital-technologies); [JWS summary](https://jws.com.au/what-we-think/harnessing-data-and-digital-technology-key-takeaways-from-the-productivity-commissions-final-report)
- **Jobs and Skills Australia (2025), *Our Gen AI Transition: Implications for Work and Skills*** (14 Aug 2025; analysis papers 2 Sep; final release 30 Sep 2025). It is the first national whole-of-labour-market Gen AI study. It finds current Gen AI more likely to augment than replace workers, especially in high-skilled occupations, and makes 10 recommendations. — [JSA report page](https://www.jobsandskills.gov.au/publications/generative-ai-capacity-study-report); [JSA study page](https://www.jobsandskills.gov.au/studies/generative-artificial-intelligence-capacity-study)

**Singapore**
- The **Enterprise Compute Initiative (ECI)**, announced in Budget 2025 (PM/FM Lawrence Wong, 18 Feb 2025), sets aside **up to S$150m**. It pairs eligible firms with major cloud providers (Google, Microsoft, AWS) for AI tools, compute and consultancy, building on the S$1b AI commitment in Budget 2024. Digital Industry Singapore describes it as a short-term programme to catalyse AI value creation. — [DISG ECI programme page](https://www.disg.gov.sg/enterprise-compute-initiative/); [EDB Budget 2025 article](https://www.edb.gov.sg/en/business-insights/insights/singapore-budget-2025-more-support-for-singapore-firms-to-tap-ai-solutions-financing.html)

### Inferences
- The US post-2019 TFP acceleration is real in the official data. Industry-level evidence suggests AI is not yet the main driver, so much of the current US advantage may come from dynamism and reallocation (Q3) and intangible deepening (Q1) rather than from measured AI diffusion.
- Australia's jump from 1% to 12% AI use is fast but from a low base. Given the frontier-diffusion evidence (Q2), the productivity payoff depends on laggard adoption and complementary intangibles, which supports a diffusion-focused rather than frontier-focused policy framing.
- Singapore (direct compute subsidies) and Australia (regulatory enablement) take contrasting approaches.

- No comparable OECD/Eurostat AI adoption rates for Canada, the EU or Singapore were retrieved (Eurostat: 2024 EU enterprise AI use is commonly cited around 13.5%, but this was not verified in this session).

## Q5. Comparators: Canada's investment-per-worker decline, Europe's tech gap, and tax/investment incentives (Australia company tax and PC proposals, Canada super/mega deduction, US bonus depreciation)

### Takeaway
Canada is the cautionary comparator. Fraser estimates Canadian business investment per worker fell from 87.3% of the US level in 2014 to 54.0% in 2024, and Ottawa has answered with escalating expensing (the 2025 Super-Deduction, then a proposed permanent Mega Deduction in September 2026). The Draghi report attributes most of the EU–US labour productivity gap (EU below 80% of US) to tech. Australia's PC proposed a hybrid: a 20% rate for firms under A$1b, 28% for larger firms in the final report, plus a 5% net cash-flow tax. The US made 100% bonus depreciation permanent under the OBBBA (2025).

### Cited Findings
**Canada**
- Fraser Institute (July 2026), *Comparing Business Investment per Worker in Canada and the United States, 2007–2024*: real business investment per worker (2017 C$) in Canada was **C$17,345 in 2007 and C$16,493 in 2024**, against US **C$19,352 in 2007 and C$30,555 in 2024**. Canada went from **87.3% of the US level in 2014 to 54.0% in 2024**. Fraser is a market-oriented think tank (label: advocacy). — [Fraser Institute study](https://www.fraserinstitute.org/studies/comparing-business-investment-per-worker-canada-and-united-states-2007-2024); [PDF](https://www.fraserinstitute.org/sites/default/files/2026-07/comparing-business-investment-per-worker-in-canada-and-united-states-2007-2024.pdf)
- Fraser (Aug 2026 update): real investment per worker in 2022–25 was lower than in 2014–21. Business non-residential investment over 2018–25 barely offset depreciation, with declines in Alberta (−2.8% a year), Saskatchewan (−1.9%) and Newfoundland and Labrador (−1.5%). — [Fraser news release](https://www.fraserinstitute.org/sites/default/files/2026-08/capital-investment-by-province-an-update-newsrelease.pdf); [Fraser study](https://www.fraserinstitute.org/studies/recent-capital-investment-not-signal-canadas-business-investment-emergency-is-over)
- C.D. Howe Institute (Robson & Bafale, Commentary 699, Dec 2025), *Canada's Investment Crisis*: business investment has been so weak since 2015 that **capital per member of the workforce is falling**, undermining labour productivity and compensation growth. The Canada–OECD gap in investment per available worker narrowed from the late 1990s to the early 2010s and has since widened, especially against the US. It cites the OECD *Economic Survey of Canada 2025* on sluggish productivity as the key factor behind stagnant living standards. — [C.D. Howe Commentary 699 (PDF)](https://cdhowe.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/Commentary_699.pdf); [C.D. Howe page](https://cdhowe.org/publication/canadas-investment-crisis-shrinking-capital-undermines-competitiveness-and-wages/)
- **Canada Budget 2025 (4 Nov 2025), Productivity Super-Deduction:** 100% immediate expensing for manufacturing and processing buildings (acquired on or after Budget Day, in use before 2030), plus M&P machinery and equipment and productivity-enhancing assets (computers, patents). It is largely a bundle of existing and restored incentives. — [Lawson Lundell](https://www.lawsonlundell.com/publication/budget-2025-key-tax-measures-and-strategic-shifts-for-canadian-businesses); [Global News](https://globalnews.ca/news/11510826/federal-budget-2025-productivity)
- **Canada Productivity Mega Deduction (proposed 15 Sep 2026):** permanent immediate expensing for most CCA property acquired on or after 15 Sep 2026. Class 1/3 buildings, Class 14/14.1 intangibles and certain vehicles are excluded. Coverage rises from about 15% of capital investment (Super-Deduction) to about two-thirds. **Fiscal cost about C$36b over five years. The government claims Canada's METR would fall from 13.0% to 6.4%, against the US at 16.9% and the OECD average at 19.0% (2026).** These are government figures, and the measure is proposed, not yet enacted. — [Department of Finance Canada news release, 15 Sep 2026](https://www.canada.ca/en/department-finance/news/2026/09/government-of-canada-introduces-new-productivity-mega-deduction-to-boost-canadas-advantage-as-the-most-competitive-g7-country-for-new-business-inve.html); [PM release](https://pm.gc.ca/en/news/news-releases/2026/09/15/prime-minister-carney-introduces-new-productivity-mega-deduction); [TD Economics commentary](https://economics.td.com/ca-productivity-mega-deduction)

**Europe**
- **Draghi (2024), *The Future of European Competitiveness*, European Commission (Sept 2024).** EU labour productivity fell from about 95% of the US level in 1995 to under 80%. The gap is "largely explained by the tech sector". The EU–US GDP gap (PPP-adjusted) rose from 15% in 2002 to 30% in 2023, with about 70% explained by productivity. The report calls for **€750–800b of additional annual investment (about 4.4–4.7% of 2023 EU GDP)**. Only 4 of the world's top 50 tech firms are European, and there is a large later-stage VC funding gap. Figures are drawn from secondary summaries. — [Tech Policy Press summary](https://techpolicy.press/draghis-european-competitiveness-report-key-findings); [Funcas](https://www.funcas.es/articulos/reforms-or-decline-what-mario-draghis-report-means-for-europe/); [RTÉ](https://www.rte.ie/news/business/2024/0909/1469106-europe-faces-existential-challenge-to-up-productivity). Primary: European Commission, https://commission.europa.eu/topics/eu-competitiveness/draghi-report_en (URL from training knowledge; not fetched).

**United States**
- The **One Big Beautiful Bill Act (2025)** permanently restored 100% bonus depreciation for short-lived assets and introduced temporary 100% expensing for certain structures (qualified production property). — [CPA Ontario](https://www.cpaontario.ca/insights/blog/what-the-one-big-beautiful-bill-act-means-for-canadian-tax); [Miller Thomson](https://www.millerthomson.com/en/insights/corporate-tax/canadas-productivity-mega-deduction-100-first-year-expensing-for-a-wide-range-of-capital-assets-is-now-on-the-table/)

**Australia**
- Current statutory rates are 25% for base-rate entities (turnover below A$50m) and 30% otherwise. PC commissioners say effective tax rates on investment are high by international standards. — [Robson & Sterland, PC, Aug 2025](https://www.pc.gov.au/media-speeches/articles/reform-company-tax)
- **PC interim report (Aug 2025):** 20% rate for companies with turnover under A$1b, plus a **5% net cash-flow tax** on all companies with immediate deduction of capex. Modelled effects: **investment +A$7.4b, GDP +A$14.6b, productivity +0.4%**, broadly revenue-neutral over the medium term. — [PC article](https://www.pc.gov.au/media-speeches/articles/reform-company-tax); [Investor Daily](https://www.investordaily.com.au/corporate-tax-reform-could-unlock-7-4bn-in-investment-for-australia-pc-report-reveals)
- **PC final report, *Creating a more dynamic and resilient economy* (released 19 Dec 2025):** keeps the 5% NCFT, with 20% for firms under A$1b and **28% for larger firms**. The PC calls the NCFT the best revenue-neutral option for investment. A headline rate cut alone would raise investment "substantially less" for the same budget cost, and if the NCFT proves too complex, alternatives include a marginal equity allowance (ACE-type) or plain rate cuts. Media reports the amended package raising GDP by about A$13b (0.7%) and investment by about 2%. These figures are secondary and unverified against the report. — [Accounting Times (media)](https://www.accountingtimes.com.au/tax/productivity-commission-calls-for-corporate-tax-and-regulatory-overhaul); [The Adviser](https://www.theadviser.com.au/growth/47941-productivity-commission-calls-for-corporate-tax-and-regulatory-overhaul); [Shepparton News (media)](https://www.sheppnews.com.au/?p=291729); [PwC Monthly Tax Update Feb 2026](https://www.pwc.com.au/tax/taxtalk/assets/monthly/pdf/monthly-tax-update-february-2026.pdf)
- Backlash: CPA Australia called the NCFT "a radical, untested experiment". CA ANZ remained concerned, including about the interaction with franking credits and foreign tax systems. Professional bodies are interested parties. — [Accounting Times](https://www.accountingtimes.com.au/tax/industry-bodies-continue-backlash-against-experimental-net-cashflow-tax); [Accountants Daily/BDO](https://www.accountantsdaily.com.au/tax-compliance/21604-bold-net-cashflow-tax-idea-could-increase-tax-complexity-bdo-says); [AAP](https://aapnews.aap.com.au/news/rethink-on-business-tax-plan-after-lobbyist-backlash)

### Inferences
- Canada shows that falling investment per worker can coexist with strong labour-force growth, as in Australia, and produce sustained productivity divergence from the US. Fraser notes Canada's workforce grew faster than the US one. That makes Canada the closest analogue to Australian "capital shallowing".
- By 2026, the US (permanent bonus depreciation) and Canada (proposed permanent broad expensing) have both moved toward cash-flow-style treatment of capex. Australia's PC proposal (a 5% NCFT layer) goes in the same direction but has not been legislated. Without reform, Australia's relative METR position likely worsens. This is an inference: I found no Australian METR estimate for 2026.
