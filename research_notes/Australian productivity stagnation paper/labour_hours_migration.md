# Labour input, hours, migration and composition effects in Australia's post-2019 productivity stagnation (vs Canada, US, Europe)

Compiled 7 November 2026. Source-quality labels: [OFFICIAL] = statistical agency or central bank or government; [PEER/WP] = peer-reviewed or staff working paper; [THINK-TANK] = independent think tank (method stated); [ADVOCACY/MEDIA] = opinion or commentary, cite only as framing. Some search tools returned summaries rather than full text. Items marked (snippet) were not read in full and should be checked before quoting.

Context supplied by the paper's own OECD analysis (not re-verified here): hours worked per head of population, 2019-2025: Australia +5.3%, Italy +9.5%, France +4.1%, Netherlands +2.2%, US -0.4%, UK -0.8%, Canada -1.9%, Germany -2.9%. Australia's GDP per capita grew 0.97%/yr over 2019-25, against 0.10%/yr for GDP per hour.

## Q1. How large was hours and employment growth 2019-2026 in Australia vs Canada, US, UK, France? Hours per capita trends

### Takeaway
Australia's labour input grew very fast after COVID. Total hours rose 12.8% and employed persons 13.2% between March 2020 and March 2025, with hours per worker roughly flat. Combined with the paper's own OECD finding (hours per head +5.3%, the second-highest in the comparison), this means Australian per-capita growth since 2019 came almost entirely from more hours per person, not from output per hour. Canada's population boom was the opposite case: hours per head fell (-1.9%) because population outran employment. In France, hours per head also rose (+4.1%), driven by job-rich growth including apprenticeships.

### Cited Findings
- **ABS Labour Account, March 2020 to March 2025:** filled jobs rose 13.7% (+1.925m), employed people 13.2% (+1.733m), hours worked 12.8% (5,285.6m to 5,961.6m hours per quarter), and labour income 38.5%. Derived hours per employed person were roughly flat (about 401.5 to 400.2, -0.3%). Multiple job-holding rose from 6.0% to 6.5% of employed persons (about 1 in 15). — [ABS (2025), "Insights into the Australian labour market", 6 June 2025](https://www.abs.gov.au/articles/insights-australian-labour-market) [OFFICIAL]
- **PC on the 2021-23 surge:** "pent-up demand, record levels of workforce participation and an increased supply of migrant workers fuelled a record surge in hours worked between September 2021 and June 2023." The PC adds that "the total supply of migrant workers since the start of the pandemic is broadly as expected." The rapid increase came after border closures, as catch-up. — [Productivity Commission (2025), *Productivity before and after COVID-19*, Research paper, May 2025, Ch. 2](https://assets.pc.gov.au/2025-10/covid-productivity-research-paper_0.pdf) [OFFICIAL]
- **RBA, Australian labour productivity:** it averaged 0.2%/yr over 2017/18-2023/24 (1.3%/yr over 1998/99-2017/18). Non-farm was 0.1%, market sector 0.6%, and market sector ex-mining 1.0% (vs 1.4%). — [Plumb, M. (2025), "Why Productivity Matters", RBA speech, ABE Forecasting Conference, 27 Feb 2025](https://www.rba.gov.au/speeches/2025/sp-so-2025-02-27.html) [OFFICIAL]
- **RBA, February 2026:** labour productivity is "currently sitting at around 2019 levels". The RBA cut its trend productivity assumption from 1.0% to 0.7%/yr in the August 2025 SMP. — [RBA (2026), Submission to the Senate Select Committee on Productivity in Australia, Feb 2026](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/submissions/labour-market-and-inflation/productivity-in-australia-02-2026/) [OFFICIAL]
- **Canada, population:** it grew 3.2% in 2023 (+1.27m), about three times the 1991-2001 pace. Real GDP per capita was 2.5% below its pre-pandemic level and 7% (about C$4,200) below its long-run trend, and real GDP was 4.4% above Q4 2019. US GDP per capita grew 1.6%/yr since the pandemic began. — [McCormack, C. & Wang, W. (2024), "Canada's gross domestic product per capita: Perspectives on the return to trend", *Economic and Social Reports* 36-28-0001, Statistics Canada, 24 Apr 2024](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/pub/36-28-0001/2024004/article/00001-eng.htm) [OFFICIAL]
- **Canada, hours:** annual hours worked fell 14.0% in 2020, then rose 11.7% (2021), 5.4% (2022), 3.3% (2023) and 1.1% (2024). Business-sector labour productivity edged up 0.2% in 2024 after three annual declines. — [Statistics Canada, *The Daily*, labour productivity tables (3 Sep 2025)](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/daily-quotidien/250903/t001a-eng.htm) (snippet) [OFFICIAL]; [Investment Executive summary of StatCan release](https://www.investmentexecutive.com/industry-news/national-productivity-edged-up-in-2024-after-declining-for-three-years/) [MEDIA]
- **Bank of Canada, newcomer arrivals:** they added about 2.5% to the level of potential output between Q3 2022 and Q1 2024, mainly through labour input. — [Bank of Canada (2024), "How newcomers impact the Canadian economy", MPR July 2024, In Focus](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/publications/mpr/mpr-2024-07/in-focus-2/) (snippet) [OFFICIAL]
- **France:** market-sector labour productivity per head was 5.2% below its Q4 2019 level in Q2 2023, and 8.5% below its 2010-19 trend (euro area: -2.4% vs trend). This happened "as job creation has outpaced GDP growth." — [Devulder, A., Ducoudré, B., Lemoine, M. & Zuber, T. (2024), "Explaining productivity losses observed in France since the pre-Covid period", *Bulletin de la Banque de France* 251/1, 11 June 2024](https://www.banque-france.fr/en/publications-and-statistics/publications/explaining-productivity-losses-observed-france-pre-covid-period) [OFFICIAL]
- **Euro area:** relative to pre-pandemic, productivity per worker had fallen about 0.8% while productivity per hour had risen about 0.6%. The gap reflects falling average hours per worker ("more jobs but fewer working hours"). — [ECB Blog (2024), 6 May 2024](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/press/blog/date/2024/html/ecb.blog20240506~f9c0c49ff7.en.html) (snippet); see also [ECB Blog (2023), "More jobs but fewer working hours", 7 June 2023](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/press/blog/date/2023/html/ecb.blog230607~9d31b379c8.en.html) [OFFICIAL]

### Inferences
- Australia's hours per worker were flat (ABS), so its hours-per-head gain (+5.3%, OECD) comes from a higher employment-to-population ratio, i.e. participation and employment rates. That contrasts with the euro area, where employment rose while average hours per worker fell, so per-worker and per-hour productivity diverge (ECB). For cross-country comparisons in the paper, per-hour measures are essential, since per-worker measures overstate the euro-area weakness.
- Canada and Australia both had migration-driven labour force booms, but they diverge on hours per head (Canada -1.9%, Australia +5.3%). Canadian population growth outpaced employment and hours; Australia's employment rate climbed to record highs. The Canadian GDP-per-capita "gap" is therefore partly an employment-rate story, while the Australian one is purely an output-per-hour story.
- Australia and France share a pattern: strong hours/employment and flat-to-negative hourly productivity. That is a "job-rich, productivity-poor" recovery, versus the US pattern of weak hours per head and strong output per hour.

### Gaps
- No single primary source found giving harmonised hours-per-capita for all eight countries over 2019-2025/26. Rely on the paper's own OECD calculation (OECD Productivity Statistics / Employment Outlook hours data).
- ABS Labour Account hours for March 2025 to mid-2026 were not retrieved. Update from ABS 6150.0.55.003 (latest quarter).
- UK (ONS) and US (BLS) hours totals for 2019-2026 were not retrieved directly.
- CSLS Research Report 2024-03 (Sharpe & Sargent, "The Canadian Productivity Landscape: an Overview") was not read in full. Its PDF is at csls.ca/pdf/csls2024-03-1.pdf ([landing page](https://csls.ca/?p=9136)).

## Q2. Evidence on COVID-era composition effects (low-productivity hours dropping out and returning)

### Takeaway
There is strong cross-country consensus that the 2020 spike in measured productivity was mostly compositional: low-productivity, low-education hours (hospitality, arts, retail) dropped out, then came back. In Australia, about 86% of the Dec 2019 to Dec 2020 rise was a between-industry effect. The 2022-23 decline, however, was mostly *within* industries (only 9% between). Labour-quality composition also matters: the RBA's real-time index shows labour quality accounting for about two-thirds of weak market-sector productivity growth over 2018/19-2023/24. It finds little evidence that inexperienced entrants explain the slump.

### Cited Findings
- **Australia, PC phases and shift-share:** the PC uses a Teo & Ong (2018) shift-share with static and dynamic between-industry effects plus a within effect. It identifies three phases: (i) "reallocation" Dec 2019 to Dec 2020, with about 86% of the productivity change due to workforce composition; (ii) "productivity gain" Dec 2020 to Mar 2022, only 10% between-industry, with 15 of 19 industries gaining; (iii) "productivity loss" Mar 2022 to Jun 2023, with productivity down 6.1% and only 9% between-industry. Only two industries (information media and telecoms; administrative and support services) kept their gains. — [Productivity Commission (2025), *Productivity before and after COVID-19*, Ch. 1](https://assets.pc.gov.au/2025-10/covid-productivity-research-paper_0.pdf) [OFFICIAL]
- **PC on the 2022-23 decline:** it attributes the fall to (a) the capital stock failing to keep pace with record hours growth (capital shallowing) and (b) younger, less experienced entrants lowering average labour quality "at least temporarily". It concludes "there are no obvious long-term implications" from COVID and that productivity "stabilised at its pre-COVID-19 level". — [PC (2025), Overview](https://assets.pc.gov.au/2025-10/covid-productivity-research-paper_0.pdf) [OFFICIAL]
- **PC on labour quality:** lockdowns hit industries with low shares of university-educated workers, raising average human capital, and this reversed as hospitality and similar industries reopened. ABS estimates attribute about one-third of market-sector labour productivity growth, and 40% of MFP growth, over 1994-95 to 2022-23 to human capital improvements. — [PC (2025), Ch. 3, citing ABS (2023)](https://assets.pc.gov.au/2025-10/covid-productivity-research-paper_0.pdf) [OFFICIAL]
- **RBA real-time labour quality index:** labour quality growth was about zero over 2016-19, rose sharply in 2020, peaked in 2021, fell slightly in 2022-23, and rose over 2024 to early 2025. On this measure, market-sector labour productivity grew about 0.3%/yr over 2018/19-2023/24, versus about 0.75%/yr in ABS headline. Labour quality accounted for about two-thirds of that labour productivity growth. In 2022/23, market-sector productivity fell 3.5% and falling labour quality subtracted only about 0.4 pp. The authors find "little to no evidence" that younger, less educated or less experienced entrants explain weak productivity. — [Bruno, A., Hambur, J. & Wang, L. (2025), "A (Closer to) Real Time Labour Quality Index", *RBA Bulletin*, July 2025](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/bulletin/2025/jul/a-closer-to-real-time-labour-quality-index.html) [OFFICIAL]
- **RBA, September 2023:** describes "a significant compositional effect, as hours were cut in low productivity sectors to a far greater extent than in higher productivity sectors." — [RBA (2023), "Recent Trends in Australian Productivity", *RBA Bulletin*, Sept 2023](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/bulletin/2023/sep/recent-trends-in-australian-productivity.html) (snippet) [OFFICIAL]
- **US, BLS:** a BLS research paper attributes much of the 2020 productivity surge to labour composition, since low-wage workers and low-wage industries shed hours and labour quality rose. — [Stewart, J. (2022), BLS Office of Survey Methods Research working paper EC220010](https://www.bls.gov/osmr/research-papers/2022/pdf/ec220010.pdf) (snippet) [PEER/WP]; also [Stewart, J. (2022), "Why Was US Labour Productivity Growth So High During the COVID-19 Pandemic?", *International Productivity Monitor* 42, CSLS](https://csls.ca/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/IPM-Issue-42-4-Stewart.pdf) (snippet) [PEER]
- **US, SF Fed:** workers with less education and experience disproportionately lost jobs, raising average education and experience. "Many of those workers returned to work and this cyclical effect on labor composition unwound." — [Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco (2024), "Productivity During and Since the Pandemic", *FRBSF Economic Letter*, Nov 2024](https://www.frbsf.org/research-and-insights/publications/economic-letter/2024/11/productivity-during-and-since-pandemic/) (snippet) [OFFICIAL]
- **US wage-composition analogue:** the CEA attributes the April 2020 jump in average wages to the loss of millions of low-paid jobs. — [Council of Economic Advisers (2021), "The Pandemic's Effect on Measured Wage Growth", 19 Apr 2021](https://bidenwhitehouse.archives.gov/cea/written-materials/2021/04/19/the-pandemics-effect-on-measured-wage-growth) [OFFICIAL]. EPI makes a similar argument ([EPI 2021](https://www.epi.org/publication/state-of-working-america-wages-in-2020/), [ADVOCACY/THINK-TANK, labour-aligned]); contrast with the [Dallas Fed (2022)](https://www.dallasfed.org/cd/communities/2022/0808) [OFFICIAL], which finds genuine low-wage earnings gains after composition adjustment.
- **UK, ONS:** labour composition (workforce skills) increased during the pandemic, because higher-skilled workers kept working while less-skilled workers were furloughed. — [ONS (2021), "Productivity overview, UK: July to September 2021"](https://www.ons.gov.uk/employmentandlabourmarket/peopleinwork/labourproductivity/articles/ukproductivityintroduction/julytoseptember2021) (snippet) [OFFICIAL]
- **Cross-country:** the composition effect was "particularly visible in the United States and Canada". — [ILOSTAT blog, "Why would labour productivity surge during a pandemic?"](https://ilostat.ilo.org/blog/why-would-labour-productivity-surge-during-a-pandemic/) [OFFICIAL/INTL ORG]

### Inferences
- For a 2019 to 2025/26 endpoint comparison, industry-composition effects largely net out, since lockdown-era shifts reversed by 2021/22. The residual structural composition effect is the secular shift to the non-market/care sector (Q4), not COVID per se.
- The RBA and PC disagree on how much weight to give inexperienced entrants. The PC treats labour-quality dilution as one of two primary drivers of the 2022-23 decline; the RBA index puts it at only about 0.4 pp of a 3.5% fall. The paper should flag the RBA estimate as the more direct measurement.
- The RBA finding that labour quality explains about two-thirds of the (weak) 2018/19-2023/24 growth implies quality-adjusted MFP and capital deepening were close to zero over the period.

### Gaps
- No ONS decomposition quantifying the UK 2019-2025 between/within split was retrieved.
- Exact BLS labour-composition contributions for 2020-2024 (BLS total factor productivity tables) were not extracted.

## Q3. Does high net overseas migration dilute capital per worker ("capital shallowing")?

### Takeaway
In both countries, official and staff work confirms the *mechanism*: rapid labour-input growth outpaced the capital stock, lowering the capital-labour ratio. The Bank of Canada explicitly models negative capital deepening in 2024 from population growth. The PC finds Australia's post-2021 capital-labour ratio fell because hours surged faster than capital. However, rigorous sources stop short of attributing Australia's stagnation *primarily* to migration. The PC says migrant supply was "broadly as expected" and blames long-term investment weakness; the RBA says capital per worker was flat for about five years pre-pandemic. Strong causal claims ("immigration is wrecking productivity") come from advocacy outlets.

### Cited Findings
- **Australia, PC capital-labour ratio:** changes during the pandemic "were mainly due to changes in hours worked". Pre-pandemic, capital growth matched hours growth. "Growth in capital is still insufficient to match increases in hours worked – a function of long term investment weakness in Australia rather than a COVID-19-specific phenomenon." It is "uncertain whether there will be a full rebound in the capital stock ... to restore the pre-pandemic level of capital per worker." The PC also cautions that ABS capital-services estimates during COVID may have overstated capital deepening in 2019-20. — [PC (2025), *Productivity before and after COVID-19*, Ch. 2](https://assets.pc.gov.au/2025-10/covid-productivity-research-paper_0.pdf) [OFFICIAL]
- **Australia, RBA:** capital per worker was "broadly unchanged for around five years before the pandemic" and is "currently a bit below those pre-pandemic levels". Slow capital deepening has contributed to slow labour productivity growth. MFP grew 0.2%/yr over 2017/18-2023/24. — [Plumb (2025), RBA speech](https://www.rba.gov.au/speeches/2025/sp-so-2025-02-27.html) [OFFICIAL]
- **Australia, RBA on entrants:** the entry of workers with fewer skills and less human capital "explains only a small share of the slowdown". — [RBA (2026), Senate submission](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/submissions/labour-market-and-inflation/productivity-in-australia-02-2026/) [OFFICIAL]
- **Australia, Treasury:** lists slow capital deepening among the structural challenges. Long-run labour productivity assumption cut from 1.5% to 1.2% (2022). No published Treasury quantification of NOM's effect on capital per worker was found. — [Treasury (2025), *Economic Reform Roundtable: Productivity overview*, Aug 2025](https://treasury.gov.au/sites/default/files/2025-08/productivity-overview.pdf) [OFFICIAL]. The 2021 IGR NOM sensitivity explicitly "does not take into account any productivity effect" of migration: [Treasury (2021), *2021 Intergenerational Report*](https://treasury.gov.au/sites/default/files/2021-06/p2021_182464.pdf) [OFFICIAL]; see also [Treasury (2023), *Intergenerational Report 2023: Australia's future to 2063*](https://treasury.gov.au/sites/default/files/2023-08/p2023-435150.pdf) [OFFICIAL].
- **Australia, e61 on migrant allocation:** migrants work disproportionately in lower-productivity industries and firms. Targeted (skilled) visa holders are linked to higher-productivity firms; student and working-holiday visa holders to lower-productivity firms. This is a composition/misallocation channel rather than capital dilution. — [e61 Institute (2023), "Misallocated migrants: Immigration and firm productivity in Australia", Mar 2023](https://e61.in/misallocated-migrants-immigration-and-firm-productivity-in-australia/) [THINK-TANK, microdata-based]
- **Australia, e61 on the gap with the US:** private-sector productivity is about 12% below the US, with almost the entire gap opening since 2016. Gianni La Cava lists skilled migration and post-study work rights among levers for absorptive capacity. — [Capital Brief (2026), reporting e61](https://www.capitalbrief.com/briefing/australias-business-productivity-12-below-us-e61-institute-f9d390c1-02bc-4998-89c8-ad4ee115cd80/) [MEDIA reporting THINK-TANK]; underlying: [e61 (2025), policy paper on Australia's key differences](https://e61.in/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/Australia_s_key_differences_policy_report.pdf) [THINK-TANK]
- **Australia, advocacy:** MacroBusiness repeatedly attributes the slump to "capital shallowing" from migration, e.g. "How Australia replaced productivity growth with immigration" (Sept 2026), "RBA admits immigration is wrecking Australia's productivity" (Mar 2025). These are opinion pieces and the RBA speech does not say this. — [MacroBusiness 2026](https://www.macrobusiness.com.au/2026/09/how-australia-replaced-productivity-growth-with-immigration/); [MacroBusiness 2025](https://www.macrobusiness.com.au/2025/03/rba-admits-immigration-is-wrecking-australias-productivity/) [ADVOCACY]
- **Canada, Bank of Canada potential output 2024:** capital deepening was "expected to remain negative in 2024 as strong population growth continues to increase TLI [trend labour input] and lower capital per unit of TLI". A 2025 rebound was expected "due to a pickup in business investment combined with a slowdown in population growth". Trend TFP was about 0.5%/yr. — [Bank of Canada (2024), "Potential output in Canada: 2024 assessment", *Staff Analytical Note* 2024-11, Apr 2024](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/2024/04/staff-analytical-note-2024-11/) [OFFICIAL]
- **Canada, Bank of Canada 2026 update:** population growth is now "subdued" given lower immigration targets. Trend labour productivity growth is projected at about 1.4%/yr, "reflecting both capital deepening and technological progress". — [Bank of Canada (2026), *Monetary Policy Report*, Apr 2026, Appendix: potential output and neutral rate](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/publications/mpr/mpr-2026-04-29/appendix/) [OFFICIAL]
- **Canada, Bank of Canada staff on composition:** analyses the shift in immigration composition (toward temporary residents). — [Bank of Canada (2025), "The Shift in Canadian Immigration Composition and its ...", Staff Discussion Paper 2025-8](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/sdp2025-8.pdf) (title truncated in search; not read) [PEER/WP]
- **Canada, Bank of Canada Senior Deputy Governor Carolyn Rogers:** called weak productivity an "emergency". Benefits from a record-expanding, immigration-driven workforce depend on skills utilisation. — [Rogers, C. (2024), "Time to break the glass: Fixing Canada's productivity problem", speech, 26 Mar 2024](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/2024/03/time-to-break-the-glass-fixing-canadas-productivity-problem/) [OFFICIAL]
- **Canada, StatCan:** fixed capital per worker was the most important source of labour productivity growth over 30 years. Investment per worker rose to 2006, then fell; by 2021 it was about 15% below 2006 (index 2006=100: 103.2 in 2015, 81.9 in 2021). Weaker competition (falling firm entry) accounts for 30% of that decline. This trend predates the 2022-24 population surge. — [McCormack & Wang (2024), Statistics Canada](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/pub/36-28-0001/2024004/article/00001-eng.htm) [OFFICIAL]. Non-residential business investment per capita was about 9% below its pre-COVID baseline (Spring 2025): [Statistics Canada (2025), "Recent developments in the Canadian economy: Spring 2025"](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/pub/36-28-0001/2025005/article/00005-eng.htm) [OFFICIAL]
- **Canada, C.D. Howe:** capital per worker has fallen since 2015; by Q3 2025 per-worker levels of all capital types were well below late-2015 levels. — [C.D. Howe Institute (2025), "Canada's Investment Crisis: Shrinking Capital Undermines Competitiveness and Wages"](https://cdhowe.org/publication/canadas-investment-crisis-shrinking-capital-undermines-competitiveness-and-wages/) [THINK-TANK, business-oriented; data-based]
- **Canada, Scotiabank:** modelling found about one-third of the negative potential-output effect of lower population growth is offset by capital-deepening productivity gains. — [Scotiabank Economics (2024), "'It's Complicated': The Macroeconomics of Canada's New Immigration Plans", 1 Nov 2024](https://www.scotiabank.com/ca/en/about/economics/economics-publications/post.other-publications.insights-views.immigration-targets--november-1--2024-.html) [PRIVATE-SECTOR RESEARCH]. The Hub "DeepDive" argues dilution of capital per worker hurt Canadian workers: [The Hub (2024)](https://thehub.ca/2024/08/19/deepdive-who-benefits-from-surging-immigration-hint-its-not-canadian-workers/) [ADVOCACY/MEDIA]
- **Theory/older Australian literature:** migration can lower capital per head, possibly offset by recent-vintage capital, with capital adjusting over time. — [Withers, G., ANU CEPR Discussion Paper No. 95, "The Economics of Immigration"](https://openresearch-repository.anu.edu.au/server/api/core/bitstreams/a11a722e-554e-4100-b730-c2cc293eacce/content) [PEER/WP, dated]; [Parliament of Australia, "The Economic Impact of Migration"](https://www.aph.gov.au/DocumentStore.ashx?id=cd4721e9-17e8-4352-b5c7-15b646a0382f&subId=350950) [OFFICIAL, dated]

### Inferences
- Rigorous work supports a *transitional* capital-shallowing effect. In the Solow logic both central banks use, a faster labour-input growth rate lowers k/L until investment catches up. The Bank of Canada quantified this as negative capital deepening in 2024; the PC shows it qualitatively for Australia over 2021-23. Neither finds a permanent effect, and both stress weak business investment as the binding constraint, which predates the migration surge (StatCan investment per worker has fallen since 2006; RBA capital per worker has been flat since about 2015).
- The two countries differ in *where* the dilution shows up. In Canada, capital per worker fell *and* employment/population fell, so GDP per capita dropped. In Australia, capital per hour fell but hours per head rose, so GDP per capita still grew about 1%/yr.
- The migration composition channel (e61: student and working-holiday visa holders concentrated in low-productivity firms; Canada's shift to temporary residents) is better evidenced than pure capital dilution and should be distinguished from it.

### Gaps
- No Treasury, RBA or Grattan paper was found that formally estimates NOM's effect on capital per worker or labour productivity for 2022-2025. Check the 2024 Population Statement and any IGR 2026 update (if released).
- Grattan Institute migration/productivity work (e.g. *Migrants in the Australian workforce*, 2024) was not retrieved in this pass.
- Bank of Canada SDP 2025-8 and CSLS work on immigration and productivity (Sharpe/Sargent) were not read in full.
- No quantification found of the ABS capital-to-hours ratio decline (in %) for 2021-2025. It could be computed from ABS 5204.0 Table 15 / 5260.0.55.002.

## Q4. Non-market sector employment growth in Australia (NDIS, health care, education, public administration): hours share and shift-share contribution

### Takeaway
The non-market sector, especially health care and social assistance, absorbed about half of Australia's labour input growth after 2020. That accounts for about 55% of filled-jobs growth and about 52.6% of hours growth over Mar 2020 to Mar 2025, with health care and social assistance contributing about 70% of the non-market share. The RBA estimates the rising non-market share lowered economy-wide productivity growth by about 0.3 pp/yr over 2017/18-2023/24, double the drag of the prior decade. The PC finds the care economy contributed 1.1 pp of the 6.1% fall in 2022-23. Both caution that measured non-market productivity is likely understated.

### Cited Findings
- **ABS, Mar 2020 to Mar 2025:** the non-market sector (health care and social assistance; education and training; public administration and safety) accounted for about 55% of filled-jobs growth and 52.6% of hours growth (ABS wording ambiguous; most likely the share of growth). Health care and social assistance accounted for about 71% of non-market job growth (+753,000 jobs) and 69.4% of non-market hours growth. Its hours rose 39.0% (+247m per quarter), employed persons 40.4% (+735,000), and labour income 51.5%. — [ABS (2025), "Insights into the Australian labour market"](https://www.abs.gov.au/articles/insights-australian-labour-market) [OFFICIAL]
- **RBA shift-share:** "the rising share of non-market employment lowered the economy-wide measure of labour productivity growth by around 0.3 percentage points per year on average from 2017/18 to 2023/24", versus about 0.15 pp/yr over the preceding decade. Measured non-market productivity may be understated, so the drag may be overstated. Market sector ex-mining productivity grew 1.0%/yr over the same period. — [Plumb (2025), RBA speech](https://www.rba.gov.au/speeches/2025/sp-so-2025-02-27.html) [OFFICIAL]
- **PC, care economy:** "a decline in labour productivity in the care economy, combined with significant growth in hours worked, has contributed 1.1 percentage points (out of 6.1%) in the fall in Australia's labour productivity between March 2022 and June 2023 – 18% of the total productivity drop". It attributes growth to the NDIS expansion (rolled out 2016-2020), aged-care standards after the Royal Commission, and early childhood education and care subsidies and workforce packages. Care-sector hours rose throughout COVID (exempt from lockdowns), and the care sector was a drag via the between effect in 2020. In 2022-23 employment growth was led by childcare and "other social assistance" (disability, aged, youth welfare); in 2023-24 it was spread roughly equally across hospitals, social assistance, medical and other health, and residential care. Footnote 15: if the decline reflects mismeasurement of quality, "the post-pandemic decline in measured productivity is exaggerated." — [PC (2025), *Productivity before and after COVID-19*, Ch. 4](https://assets.pc.gov.au/2025-10/covid-productivity-research-paper_0.pdf) [OFFICIAL]

- **Public sector, 2025-26:** health care and social assistance was the fastest-growing public-sector industry (+29,700 jobs, +4.4%). — [PS News (2026)](https://psnews.com.au/health-care-and-social-work-lead-slow-growth-in-public-sector-jobs/188170/) [MEDIA reporting ABS]
- **PC Deputy Chair Alex Robson:** non-market productivity is below its March 2007 level (as reported). — [AAP (2025)](https://aapnews.aap.com.au/news/rising-rates-bond-yields-call-for-deeper-spending-cuts) [MEDIA]. - **RBA labour-quality work:** excludes the non-market sector because output is measured largely by inputs, so the wage-equals-productivity assumption is weaker there. — [Bruno, Hambur & Wang (2025)](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/bulletin/2025/jul/a-closer-to-real-time-labour-quality-index.html) [OFFICIAL]

### Inferences
- A rough cumulative estimate: 0.3 pp/yr × 6 years ≈ 1.8 pp of lost measured productivity from sectoral reallocation alone over 2017/18-2023/24 (RBA estimate scaled up; my calculation). Against total labour productivity growth of about 0.2%/yr, the non-market shift accounts for a large share of the gap to the 1.3%/yr historical average (about 0.3 of about 1.1 pp).
- Because non-market output is largely input-measured (by hours or deflated wages), its measured labour productivity growth is near zero by construction. The shift-share drag is therefore partly a measurement artefact, and the paper should present market-sector ex-mining figures (1.0%/yr) alongside the aggregate figures.
- This is a distinctive Australian factor relative to Canada and the US. It reflects policy-driven demand (NDIS, aged care, childcare subsidies) rather than market forces. Its labour was partly supplied by migrants, which links Q3 and Q4.

- No exact non-market share of total hours (level) for 2019 vs 2025/26 was retrieved. Compute from the ABS Labour Account industry summary table.
- No comparable non-market shift-share estimates were retrieved for Canada, the UK or the US.

## Q5. Labour hoarding and tight labour markets (ESM, ECB on euro area; RBA on Australia); France apprenticeship effect

### Takeaway
Labour hoarding is a well-documented driver of weak euro-area per-worker productivity over 2022-24. The ESM estimates Germany's employment about 7% and France's about 5% above activity-consistent levels in Q2 2024. For France, the Banque de France decomposes the 8.5% per-head productivity shortfall: apprenticeships 1.2 pp, workforce composition 1.4 pp, lockdown effects on potential 0.4 pp (permanent, about 3.1 pp in total), labour hoarding 1.7 pp (temporary), with about 3.6 pp unexplained. No RBA analysis explicitly framing Australian weakness as labour hoarding was found; Australian labour tightness is instead framed via hours growth outpacing capital and via job mobility.

### Cited Findings
- **ESM:** labour hoarding is significant in Germany (employment about 7% above no-hoarding levels) and France (about 5%), "significantly" driving the euro-area phenomenon. Euro-area excess employment is about 2.1% (about 3.6m workers) as of Q2 2024. It is concentrated in industry (employment 9.3% above activity-predicted, equal to 1.3% of total euro-area employment) and construction; services about 2.3%. The normal cyclical productivity recovery pattern "has broken down". — [Gabriele, C., Gumanova, K. & Kühl, M. (2024), "Labour hoarding – euro area's Achilles heel?", ESM Blog, 30 Oct 2024](https://www.esm.europa.eu/blog/labour-hoarding-euro-areas-achilles-heel); [PDF](https://www.esm.europa.eu/system/files/document/2024-10/Labour%20hoarding%20euro%20areas%20achilles%20heal.pdf) [OFFICIAL]; sector figures via [Paperjam](https://en.paperjam.lu/article/labour-hoarding-poses-multiple) [MEDIA]
- **ECB:** productivity growth has fallen since 2022. The ECB labour-hoarding indicator has been above its historical average since Q1 2022 but started weakening in 2024. — [ECB (2024), "Explaining the resilience of the euro area labour market between 2022 and 2024", *Economic Bulletin* Issue 8/2024, article 2](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/press/economic-bulletin/articles/2025/html/ecb.ebart202408_02~8e16d5aa2f.en.html) [OFFICIAL]
- **ECB, 2026:** signs that firms' labour hoarding, high during the pandemic and energy shock, has declined. — [ECB Blog (2026), "Low unemployment, plenty of labour: what does it imply for wage pressures?", 9 Mar 2026](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/press/blog/date/2026/html/ecb.blog20260309~3a4841b028.en.html) (snippet) [OFFICIAL]
- **ECB, consumption:** lower workloads from hoarding raise precautionary saving and weigh on consumption. — [ECB Blog (2025), "Those who work less worry more: the effect of lower workloads on consumption", 20 Feb 2025](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/press/blog/date/2025/html/ecb.blog20250220~540b80f133.en.html) [OFFICIAL]. Longer treatment: [ECB Working Paper No. 3180, "The labour market in the euro area: and yet, it moves!"](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/pub/pdf/scpwps/ecb.wp3180~6e06816794.en.pdf) (not read) [PEER/WP]
- **Firm-level:** a 1 pp higher profit margin raises the probability a firm hoards labour by 0.2 pp (as reported in ECB-linked work). — [ECB Economic Bulletin 8/2024](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/press/economic-bulletin/articles/2025/html/ecb.ebart202408_02~8e16d5aa2f.en.html) (snippet). Eurostat survey data suggest about 15% of firms in Germany and France were hoarding, about 5 pp above pre-pandemic — [Bergeaud, A. (2024), "The Past, Present and Future of European Productivity", ECB Forum on Central Banking, Sintra 2024](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/pub/pdf/sintra/ecb.forumcentbankpub2024_Bergeaud_paper.en.pdf) [PEER/CONFERENCE]. Survey-based EU measure: [VoxEU/CEPR, "Vulnerabilities of the labour market: A new survey-based measure of labour hoarding in the EU"](https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/vulnerabilities-labour-market-new-survey-based-measure-labour-hoarding-eu) [PEER/COLUMN]
- **France, Banque de France decomposition:** of the 8.5% per-head productivity gap vs the 2010-19 trend (market sectors, Q2 2023), apprenticeships account for 1.2 pp, workforce composition (larger rise in less-qualified employment) 1.4 pp, and lockdown/COVID effects on potential 0.4 pp, about 3.1 pp permanent in total. Labour hoarding accounts for 1.7 pp ("the lion's share" of 1.8 pp temporary). Sick leave and short-time work no longer contribute materially. The explained total is about 4.9 pp, leaving about 3.6 pp unexplained (my derivation, consistent with Rexecode's "4.9" summary). Apprentices' hourly productivity is about 2.8 times lower than other employees'. Much of the loss reflects higher "employment intensity" of growth, a switch between productivity and labour, rather than lost potential. — [Devulder, Ducoudré, Lemoine & Zuber (2024), *Bulletin de la Banque de France* 251/1](https://www.banque-france.fr/en/publications-and-statistics/publications/explaining-productivity-losses-observed-france-pre-covid-period); [PDF](https://www.banque-france.fr/system/files/2024-06/284024_BDF251_1_Pertes_prod-EN_VF.pdf) [OFFICIAL]; summary [Rexecode](https://www.rexecode.fr/conjoncture-previsions/archives/document-de-la-semaine/la-banque-de-france-recherche-les-causes-de-la-chute-de-la-productivite-depuis-la-crise-covid) [THINK-TANK]. Note: figures were revised in late May 2024, so cite the June 2024 version.
- **Banque de France companion:** "A measure of efficiency in the use of labour resources: beyond productivity". — [Banque de France](https://www.banque-france.fr/en/publications-and-statistics/publications/measure-efficiency-use-labour-resources-beyond-productivity) (not read) [OFFICIAL]
- **Australia:** the PC notes the JobKeeper wage subsidy kept workers attached to specific jobs, which "limited worker mobility, firm entry and exit". It "may have prevented the type of productivity-enhancing movements of firms and people observed in other economies, such as the US", while avoiding US-style job losses. — [PC (2025), Overview](https://assets.pc.gov.au/2025-10/covid-productivity-research-paper_0.pdf) [OFFICIAL]. The RBA's Feb 2026 submission does not mention labour hoarding — [RBA (2026)](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/submissions/labour-market-and-inflation/productivity-in-australia-02-2026/) [OFFICIAL]

### Inferences
- Labour hoarding is a cyclical, per-worker phenomenon concentrated in German and French industry. It operates partly through lower hours per worker, so it shows up more in per-worker than per-hour productivity. Australia's pattern (flat hours per worker, record employment rate) looks more like strong demand plus supply expansion than hoarding against weak demand.
- The French case is the closest European analogue to Australia: both had policy-driven low-productivity employment growth (apprenticeship subsidies in France, care-sector funding in Australia) that raises hours per head and lowers measured productivity. In both, central-bank analysis argues much of the loss is an employment-intensity trade-off rather than lost potential.
- JobKeeper-style retention schemes (Australia), Kurzarbeit (Germany) and activité partielle (France) all plausibly suppressed post-COVID reallocation relative to the US.

### Gaps
- INSEE's own decomposition of French productivity losses was not retrieved. INSEE *Note de conjoncture* boxes, 2023-24, estimate apprenticeship effects similar to the Banque de France's.
- No RBA or ABS estimate of Australian labour hoarding was found. The search did not locate any.
- The Bundesbank's view on German hoarding was not retrieved.

## Q6. Labour market dynamism, job-to-job mobility and reallocation (Andrews & Hambur; Deutscher; e61; US post-COVID)

### Takeaway
Australian business and labour dynamism have declined secularly: lower firm entry/exit, and job switching falling from about 11.3% in the mid-2000s to about 8.5% in 2019. Post-COVID switching only briefly recovered (about 9.5% in 2023). e61 estimates reduced productivity-enhancing reallocation accounts for about one-quarter of the productivity slowdown. RBA work finds the 2022 mobility spike did not damage match quality. The US contrasted sharply: a pandemic surge in business formation and quits (Decker & Haltiwanger). The PC links this to JobKeeper limiting reallocation in Australia.

### Cited Findings
- **RBA:** "Business and labour market dynamism has declined, with firm entry and exit rates falling and people becoming less likely to change jobs". Much of the switching decline reflects fewer moves to new or young firms, slowing reallocation to more productive firms. — [RBA (2026), Senate submission](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/submissions/labour-market-and-inflation/productivity-in-australia-02-2026/) [OFFICIAL]
- **Hambur & Andrews:** declining dynamism leaves labour "increasingly 'trapped' in lower productivity firms"; capital misallocation also plays a role. — [Hambur, J. & Andrews, D. - **Hambur, wages:** declining job-to-job and especially occupational switching may have held back wage growth, both via productivity and via bargaining power. — [Hambur, J. (2023), "Did Labour Market Concentration Lower Wages Growth Pre-COVID?", RBA RDP 2023-02](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/rdp/2023/2023-02/full.html) [PEER/WP]

- **Andrews & Hansell:** productivity-enhancing labour reallocation in Australia — [Andrews, D. & Hansell, D. (2021), *Economic Record* 97(317): 157-169](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/submissions/labour-market-and-inflation/productivity-in-australia-02-2026/) (citation from the RBA reference list) [PEER]

### Inferences
- The divergence between Australia and the US in post-COVID productivity is consistent with differing reallocation regimes. The US had large job destruction, then a surge in business formation and quits; Australia had job retention (JobKeeper) and a brief, modest mobility uptick. The PC explicitly draws this contrast, though it does not quantify the productivity effect.
- e61's "one-quarter of the slowdown" figure is a long-run (mid-2000s onward) attribution, not specific to 2019-26. Combined with the RBA's ≈0.3 pp/yr non-market drag and weak capital deepening, these three factors plausibly cover most of the observed shortfall, but they are not additive and come from different methods.

- No US BLS/JOLTS job-to-job figures for 2019-2026 were extracted, and no quantitative link between US reallocation and the 2023-25 US productivity rebound was found in this pass.
- No Canadian, UK or French job-mobility comparators were retrieved.
