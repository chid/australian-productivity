## 1. Mining MFP: depletion, ore grades, investment-to-production lags; PC and ABS estimates

### Takeaway
Mining MFP fell 3.3% in 2024-25 according to the ABS. That was its fifth straight annual decline, and over 2001-02 to 2023-24 mining was the second-worst industry, with cumulative MFP of about -34%. PC work going back to Topp et al. (2008) breaks this decline into falling resource yields (depletion and ore grades) and production lags after big capital investment. Once those are removed, underlying MFP was positive.

### Cited Findings
- **ABS 2024-25 (released 6 Feb 2026):** mining MFP was **-3.3%** on an hours-worked basis, the fifth consecutive annual decline and the largest fall of any market-sector industry. The ABS puts it down to "production disruptions associated with poor weather". Market-sector MFP was -0.5% (hours-worked basis) and -0.8% (quality-adjusted labour input basis). Construction MFP was -2.8%, EGWW (utilities) -0.1%, and agriculture +9.9%, the largest rise. — [ABS, Estimates of Industry Multifactor Productivity, 2024-25](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/industry/industry-overview/estimates-industry-multifactor-productivity/latest-release)
  - **Discrepancy:** the brief says "-3.2%". The ABS release page gives **-3.3%**. Use -3.3% unless a later revision or a PC restatement of -3.2% can be located. I found no source for -3.2%.
- Mining's direct contribution to 2024-25 market-sector labour productivity growth was **-1.0 percentage points**, partly offset by **+0.7 pp from hours reallocation** (search summary of the ABS release). — [ABS 2024-25 release](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/industry/industry-overview/estimates-industry-multifactor-productivity/latest-release)
- Mining MFP fell **2.3% in 2023-24**, which was then its fourth consecutive decline (trade press citing ABS). — [miningday.com.au (trade press)](https://www.miningday.com.au/?p=34403)
- **Cumulative MFP change, 2001-02 to 2023-24:** mining **-34%**, second-worst after utilities (-35%) [verified in primary doc: PC chart data XLSX, Fig. 1a]. — [PC, Quarterly productivity bulletin – June 2026, chart data](https://assets.pc.gov.au/2026-08/quarterly-bulletin-june-2026-chart-data.xlsx)
- **Topp, Soames, Parham & Bloch (2008), PC Staff Working Paper** [verified in primary doc]:
  - Mining usually makes up about 5% of nominal market-sector GDP. That rose to **8.5% in 2006-07** during the "once-in-a-generation" commodity shock.
  - Mining MFP **fell 24% between 2000-01 and 2006-07**.
  - "Yield declines and a surge in new capital investment are estimated to have contributed substantially to the decline". Yield declines dominate the early years of the period, and production lags from the 2004-05 to 2006-07 investment surge dominate the later years.
  - "After removing the influence of yield changes and production lags, other factors are estimated to have **raised mining MFP by 8 per cent** over the period."
  - Mining MFP fell again by **just under 8% in 2007-08**, and capital-investment lags explain **about 5 pp** of that fall. Data limits ruled out quantifying depletion for 2007-08, but falling crude oil and condensate flow rates suggest depletion contributed.
  - Elsewhere the paper reports an 8.8% fall in mining MFP in 2005-06, which took almost 1 pp off market-sector MFP growth.
  - The paper includes ore-grade series for iron ore, copper, gold and silver/lead/zinc from 1971-72 to 2006-07.
  - Source: [Topp, V., Soames, L., Parham, D. & Bloch, H. (2008). *Productivity in the Mining Industry: Measurement and Interpretation*. Productivity Commission Staff Working Paper, December, Canberra](https://assets.pc.gov.au/research/supporting/mining-productivity/mining-productivity.pdf); RePEc handle [ris:prodsw:0807](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:ris:prodsw:0807)
- **ABS experimental natural-resource-adjusted mining MFP:** the December 2014 release (5260.0.55.002, 2013-14) introduced experimental mining MFP that "accounts for changes in mineral and energy resources inputs" (Table 24). Core tables 1 to 19 continued without resource inputs. Subsoil assets are valued by net present value because "there are insufficient transactions in subsoil natural resources". Including them "significantly moderates mining capital services growth and thus moderates the decline in mining multifactor productivity". — [ABS 5260.0.55.002, 2013-14, Feature Article](https://www.abs.gov.au/ausstats/abs@.nsf/Lookup/5260.0.55.002Feature+Article12013-14); background in [ABS 5260.0.55.001 Information paper: Experimental Estimates of Industry MFP, 2007](https://www.abs.gov.au/AusStats/ABS@.nsf/Latestproducts/B34D22CD2A86EEC7CA25734E0019DEEF), which noted that subsoil asset services were "not currently included in the capital services measure for Mining".
- The PC (June 2026) explicitly compares the electricity sector's experience to "the 'investment phase' of a mining boom". In its words, "measured productivity falls while companies are building new mines (increasing inputs) that they cannot yet use (keeping output the same). Productivity then rises as the mining boom moves into its 'production phase'" [verified in primary doc]. — [PC Quarterly bulletin June 2026](https://assets.pc.gov.au/2026-06/quarterly-bulletin-june-2026.pdf)

### Inferences
- The 2024-25 fall was weather-driven, which is a different cause from the 2000s decline (depletion plus lags). A paper should not attribute all post-2019 mining weakness to depletion. The most recent years combine weather, falling grades (especially gold and copper), and continuing LNG and lithium capacity that has yet to reach full output.
- Mining enters the economy-wide MFP decomposition through two channels. One is a direct within-industry drag. The other is a reallocation effect, which shows up as a positive hours-reallocation term because mining's labour productivity is high. The paper should report both.
- The ABS resource-adjusted series is the natural robustness check for a measured mining MFP of -34%. I could not confirm whether the ABS still publishes it as Table 24 or an equivalent in 2024-25. The methodology page lists experimental Tables 20 to 26 without naming the series.

### Gaps
- I found no source for "mining MFP -3.2% in 2024-25". The ABS figure is -3.3%.
- I could not confirm whether the experimental resource-adjusted mining MFP is still published, or what its latest value is.
- I did not retrieve RBA Bulletin articles on the mining investment-to-production transition or productivity, as I ran out of search budget. Suggested follow-up: search the RBA Bulletin for "mining sector productivity" and "mining investment boom phases".
- I found no recent (2020s) PC or ABS quantification of ore-grade decline contributions. Topp et al. (2008) is the latest decomposition I located.

## 2. Utilities/electricity: the PC June 2026 feature, AER benchmarking, quality- and emissions-adjusted productivity, and renewables timing

### Takeaway
The PC's June 2026 bulletin feature finds utilities to be the worst industry for cumulative MFP from 2001-02 to 2023-24, at -35%. Its experimental electricity estimate is also about -35%, with capital stock up **126%** against GVA up only **14%**. The PC argues that much of this is investment lag, replacement of coal plant, lower renewable capacity factors, and unmeasured gains in quality, reliability and emissions. Adjusting for quality (AER) and emissions roughly halves to two-thirds the measured decline.

### Cited Findings (all from PC 2026 [verified in primary doc] unless noted)
Primary source: [Productivity Commission (2026). *Quarterly productivity bulletin – June 2026*, PC productivity insights, Canberra, released 11 June 2026. Feature article: "Electricity industry productivity: what the numbers miss"](https://assets.pc.gov.au/2026-06/quarterly-bulletin-june-2026.pdf). The web page is [here](https://www.pc.gov.au/ongoing/productivity-insights/bulletins/quarterly-bulletin-june-2026), the chart data [here](https://assets.pc.gov.au/2026-08/quarterly-bulletin-june-2026-chart-data.xlsx) and Appendix B [here](https://assets.pc.gov.au/2026-06/quarterly-bulletin-june-2026-appendixb.pdf). Media release attributed to Deputy Chair Dr Alex Robson. The bulletin names no individual authors.

**Industry MFP table, cumulative change 2001-02 to 2023-24** (PC calculations based on ABS 2026a and BLADE; Fig. 1a chart data):

| Industry | Cumulative MFP change |

- **Indices for 2023-24 (2001-02 = 100):** utilities 65.0, electricity (experimental) 65.0, market sector 108.5. Electricity MFP was roughly flat to 2008-09 (99.6), then fell steeply to 81.1 by 2013-14. It recovered partly to 85.9 in 2015-16 and then fell to 65.0 by 2023-24.
- **Inputs and outputs for electricity, 2001-02 to 2023-24 (Fig. 3):** "Despite a **126% increase in the capital stock** since 2001–02, output has only increased **14%**." In the chart data, the capital index is 226.1, the GVA index 114.2 and the labour index 124.2 in 2024. Capital is an experimental perpetual-inventory estimate apportioned from utilities using BLADE and Business Activity Statement capital-purchase shares, which the PC calls "illustrative but not precise" (App. A.2).
- **Capital intensity:** in 2024 the system needed "about **$10 of capital for every $1 of output**", against an economy-wide average of "just over $2".
- Electricity accounts for **57% of value added within utilities**.
- **Definitions:** electricity industry output includes rooftop solar generation. Construction of new generation and transmission is counted as construction-industry output, not electricity output.
- **Three reasons for the transformation:** replacing ageing coal assets; quality and reliability, including peak-demand capacity, safety, Victoria's smart-meter rollout and the Heywood interconnector upgrade; and the shift to cleaner energy.
- **Renewables share and timing:** "approximately **37% of investment since 2017–18** has been in new renewable generation and storage". From the chart data, renewable energy construction rose from $0.87b in 2016-17 to $8.37b in 2023-24, and other energy construction from $7.13b to $13.25b. Total energy-related construction was about $21.6b in 2023-24.
- **Capacity factors:** CSIRO GenCost 2025-26 assumes **19%** for large-scale solar PV (low-cost scenario) against **89%** for new black coal (Graham & Hayward 2025, p. 73). Lower capacity factors push up capital per unit of output.
- **Counterfactual prices:** Simshauser & Gilmore (2026) argue that NEM wholesale prices would be higher today had investment been limited to coal and gas.
- **Quality adjustment using AER benchmarking:** from 2006-07 to 2023-24, AER quality-adjusted productivity "fell **8%** for the distribution element … and **15%** for the transmission element, compared to **24% and 26%** falls in the non-adjusted productivity measures" (Fig. 4; PC calculations based on [AER 2025a/2025b Annual benchmarking reports](https://www.aer.gov.au/) and Quantonomics 2025). Peak-to-average demand rose with air-conditioning in the 2000s and later with rooftop solar, which lowered average utilisation (AER 2025a, p. 8).
- **Emissions adjustment:** electricity-sector CO2 is down **30% from its 2008-09 peak**. Using a target-consistent carbon value of **$67/t** (Treasury's marginal abatement incentive for 2025 to 2030), electricity productivity "has fallen by **23% rather than 32%** between 2001–02 and 2022–23" (App. A.3). The method follows De Ridder & Rachel (2025, LSE), with emissions-adjusted productivity = productivity × (1 − emissions × SCC / output).
- **Three MFP measures compared (App. A.1):** ABS utilities MFP (labour plus capital; national accounts), PC electricity MFP (labour plus capital; national accounts plus BLADE), and AER MFP for transmission and distribution (capital plus opex; regulatory data; NEM only).
- **Policy, drawing on PC 2025 inquiry report no. 113:** technology-neutral incentives would cut the cost of meeting state renewable targets by **4%** over 2026 to 2040. Federal rather than state targets would cut it by **8%**. A one-year acceleration of approvals for wind farms and key transmission "could cut electricity bills by **7%** over a decade". The PC also recommends a clean-energy approvals strike team and an Australian Government Coordinator-General. — [PC (2025). *Investing in cheaper, cleaner energy and the net zero transformation*, Inquiry Report no. 113, Canberra](https://www.pc.gov.au/)
- Earlier PC methodological reference: [Topp, V. & Kulys, T. (2012). *Productivity in Electricity, Gas and Water: Measurement and Interpretation*. PC Staff Working Paper, Canberra](https://assets.pc.gov.au/research/supporting/electricity-gas-water/electricity-gas-water.pdf). It notes that underground cabling reduces bushfire and car-crash risk, a benefit no productivity measure captures (pp. 53-54).
- **Commentary and advocacy, label if used:** The Spectator's "Flat White" column used the 126% and 14% figures to criticise the transition. — [The Spectator Australia (opinion)](https://www.spectator.com.au/?p=10936043)
- The Queensland Productivity Commission has published separate advice on energy productivity (2025). — [QPC Advice on energy productivity and policy 2025](https://www.treasury.qld.gov.au/files/QPC-Advice-on-energy-productivity-and-policy-2025.pdf) (not read in detail)

### Inferences
- The "-35%" figure is effectively the same for utilities and electricity, so the electricity sub-sector drives the utilities result: it is 57% of value added and has the same trajectory.
- The PC's decomposition gives roughly three layers. The measured decline is about -32% to -35%. Emissions-adjusted, it is about -23%. For networks specifically, AER quality adjustment reduces the decline by roughly two-thirds in distribution (-24% to -8%) and by about 40% in transmission (-26% to -15%). The two adjustments apply to different scopes and periods, so they cannot simply be summed.
- The 2008-09 to 2013-14 collapse in electricity MFP coincides with the network investment surge in the NSW and Queensland government-owned DNSPs. This is the "gold-plating" era analysed in the regulatory literature. The 2017-18 onward decline coincides with renewables and storage investment. The two phases need separate treatment.

### Gaps
- **Network "gold-plating":** I did not retrieve primary sources in this session. Known leads, unverified here: Grattan Institute (2013), *Shock to the system* and (2018) *Down to the wire* (Wood et al.); PC (2013) *Electricity Network Regulatory Frameworks* inquiry report; Mountain (2011+) on network over-investment. Pull full bibliographic details before citing.
- I did not read the AER 2025 benchmarking reports or Quantonomics directly. The figures above are the PC's restatement.
- I did not obtain AEMO ISP (2024/2026) build-out timing data.
- The AER chart-data dump I retrieved ended at 2021-22 because my extraction was truncated. Index values for 2022-23 and 2023-24 are in the XLSX.

## 3. Construction productivity in Australia and housing supply

### Takeaway
The PC's 2025 research paper finds that housing construction productivity measured in physical terms (dwellings completed per hour worked) fell **53%** over about 30 years. Value-based labour productivity fell 12%, while whole-economy labour productivity rose 49%. Construction MFP was -8% cumulatively from 2001-02 to 2023-24, and -2.8% in 2024-25 alone. The PC links this to slow approvals, little innovation, a fragmented small-firm structure, and workforce constraints, and so to housing supply.

### Cited Findings
- **PC (2025), *Housing construction productivity: Can we fix it?*, Research paper, February 2025** [verified in primary doc]:
  - "the number of dwellings completed per hour worked by housing construction workers has declined by **53%**". Value-based labour productivity, which captures quality improvements and larger homes, "has declined by **12%**". Whole-economy labour productivity rose **+49%** over the same period. Had the economy matched housing construction, "average incomes in Australia would be about **41% lower**".
  - Detached-house construction labour productivity has fallen about **25% since 2001-02**. Higher-density construction (townhouses, units, apartments) **rose 5%** over the same period. Note: I read this from an extracted text fragment. Confirm the exact wording on the report's p. 2 or 3.
  - Only **35%** of construction firms are "innovation-active". The **top four firms' market share was 12% in 2017**, the lowest of any sector.
  - Higher-density housing went from 3% of new builds in 1955 to about a third now. Annualised completion growth was about 0.6%/yr for detached houses against 4.8% for higher-density.
  - Source: [Productivity Commission (2025). *Housing construction productivity: Can we fix it?* Research paper, Canberra](https://assets.pc.gov.au/research/completed/housing-construction/housing-construction.pdf)
- The four constraints identified are complex and slow approvals, lack of innovation, an industry dominated by small firms, and difficulty attracting and retaining workers. There are seven reform directions, including a review of the National Construction Code (search summaries). — [UTS news (2025)](https://www.uts.edu.au/news/2025/02/productivity-housing-construction-tricky-problem); [ArchitectureAU](https://architectureau.com/articles/heres-why-increasing-productivity-in-housing-construction-is-such-a-tricky-problem-to-solve/)
- ABS construction MFP was **-2.8% in 2024-25**. — [ABS 2024-25](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/industry/industry-overview/estimates-industry-multifactor-productivity/latest-release)
- Construction's cumulative MFP was **-8%** from 2001-02 to 2023-24 (PC Fig. 1a chart data) [verified]. — [PC June 2026 chart data](https://assets.pc.gov.au/2026-08/quarterly-bulletin-june-2026-chart-data.xlsx)
- Queensland Productivity Commission interim report (Aug 2025): Queensland construction productivity is down about **9% since 2018**. — [Queensland Treasury newsroom](https://www.treasury.qld.gov.au/newsroom/interim-report-reveals-drop-in-construction-productivity/)
- **Dissent (blog, non-peer-reviewed):** Fresh Economic Thinking (Cameron Murray) disputes the PC's methodology and argues the evidence is unclear. — [Fresh Economic Thinking](https://www.fresheconomicthinking.com/p/despite-what-youve-heard-we-dont)
- Industry and advocacy reactions, labelled as such: [Property Council](https://www.propertycouncil.com.au/property-australia/productivity-commissions-report-a-love-letter-to-housing-supply); [Master Builders WA](https://www.mbawa.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/2025.02.17-Productivity-Commission-report-reinforces-Industry-calls-for-reform.pdf)
- The PC's electricity feature notes that new generation and transmission build is counted as **construction** output [verified]. Energy-transition construction (about $21.6b in 2023-24) therefore competes with housing for construction labour.

### Inferences
- The gap between the physical measure (-53%) and the value measure (-12%) reflects larger and higher-quality dwellings and the composition shift. Physical-unit measures overstate the decline, and the critics' objection is mostly about this.
- Detached-house productivity falling while higher-density productivity rises points to regulation and site-level constraints rather than economy-wide technology. It also suggests that zoning reform that moves the mix toward medium density could raise measured productivity.

### Gaps
- I did not verify the link between construction productivity and housing supply elasticity or affordability (for example RBA RDP 2018-03 by Kendall & Tulip on zoning).
- The OECD Economic Survey of Australia (2025/2026) treatment of construction and housing was not retrieved.

## 4. Germany/Europe: the energy price shock and industrial productivity

### Takeaway
IMF work attributes Germany's growth downturn about 60% to lower potential growth and 40% to cyclical factors, with **productivity declines in industry and construction** rather than hours. The 2022 energy shock is an amplifier rather than the main cause. The Draghi report puts EU industrial electricity prices at 2 to 3 times those of the US and China, and gas at 3 to 5 times US levels.

### Cited Findings
- **Kemp, H. (2026). *Drivers of Germany's Growth Downturn*. IMF Working Paper WP/26/112, June 2026:**
  - About 60% of Germany's growth underperformance is lower potential growth and about 40% is cyclical.
  - The recession is concentrated in manufacturing and construction.
  - The output decline is driven mainly by falling productivity, especially in industry and construction, not by fewer hours worked.
  - One-off effects of the 2022 energy-price shock are listed among the amplifying factors, alongside structural problems and weaker external competitiveness.
  - Source: [IDEAS/RePEc imf/imfwpa/2026-112](https://ideas.repec.org/p/imf/imfwpa/2026-112.html); [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:imf:imfwpa:2026/112). Abstract only. The author's name and these details come from the search summary, so verify on the IMF site.
- **IMF WP/26/003 (Lan, Patnam, Toscani & Li, 2026):** the post-2022 change in European energy prices lowers the euro-area potential GDP *level* by **0.8% by 2027**. The effect on potential growth is temporary. — [IDEAS imf/imfwpa/2026-003](https://ideas.repec.org/p/imf/imfwpa/2026-003.html)
- **IMF Selected Issues Paper SIP/2023/059, "Impact of High Energy Prices on Germany's Potential Output" (July 2023):** energy prices 20% above the 2018-19 average reduce German potential output by **1.2%** in the medium term. — [IMF SIP 2023/059](https://www.imf.org/en/Publications/selected-issues-papers/Issues/2023/07/24/Impact-of-High-Energy-Prices-on-Germanys-Potential-Output-536837)
- **Draghi, M. (2024). *The Future of European Competitiveness*, European Commission, September 2024:** EU industrial electricity prices are 2 to 3 times those in the US and China, and gas prices are 3 to 5 times US levels (Draghi's EP remarks). Energy is a key factor in the competitiveness gap. Proposals include low-cost generation portfolios for energy-intensive industry, joint LNG procurement, network tariff rebates and state aid. — [Carbon Brief Q&A](https://www.carbonbrief.org/qa-what-the-draghi-report-says-about-eu-climate-action-and-energy/); report at [commission.europa.eu](https://commission.europa.eu/topics/eu-competitiveness/draghi-report_en)
- Bruegel criticises Draghi's energy proposals as favouring energy-intensive industry "without clear justification". — [Bruegel First Glance](https://www.bruegel.org/first-glance/draghis-pitch-improve-competitiveness-energy-intensive-industry)
- Oxford Institute for Energy Studies (Oct 2026), *SP38: Revisiting the Energy Findings and Recommendations of Draghi's Report*. This is a current follow-up that I did not read. — [OIES SP38](https://www.oxfordenergy.org/wpcms/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/SP38-Revisiting-the-Energy-Findings-and-Recommendations-of-Draghis-Report.pdf)

### Inferences
- The German case is the mirror image of Australia's. In Germany a *price* shock to an energy-importing manufacturing base lowered measured productivity through utilisation, output loss in energy-intensive industry, and reallocation. In Australia a *capital-deepening* transition lowered measured utilities MFP. Both depress measured MFP, through different mechanisms.
- Construction productivity weakness appears in both Germany and Australia.

### Gaps
- I did not read the full text of WP/26/112, so I cannot quantify how much of Germany's productivity decline is energy-related.
- I did not retrieve sectoral evidence on energy-intensive output in Germany (chemicals, metals; Destatis), or on European productivity effects of the shock (ECB, Bruegel quantitative work).

## 5. Canada and Norway: resource-sector productivity, Dutch disease, and resource rents in measured productivity

### Takeaway
Canada shows the same mechanism as Australian mining. High prices made high-cost oil sands profitable while measured MFP in mining and oil and gas fell sharply. Norway's OECD narrative stresses weak *mainland* productivity growth eroding its income lead, with petroleum supporting demand but not productivity.

### Cited Findings
- **Canada, oil and gas extraction:** labour productivity fell **8.23%/yr** and capital productivity **5.97%/yr** between the 2000 peak and 2007. Because of price and share effects, the industry still contributed positively to aggregate labour productivity growth over 2000 to 2006. — Sharpe, A. & Bradley, C., Centre for the Study of Living Standards (CSLS Research Report 2009-08). [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/paper/slsresrep/0908.htm)
- A Globe and Mail column citing StatCan says MFP in mining and oil and gas extraction **fell by about a third since 2002**. This is a secondary source; verify against the StatCan tables. — [Globe and Mail](https://beta.theglobeandmail.com/report-on-business/economy/economy-lab/forget-productivity-our-incomes-are-rising/article617089/)
- **Alberta:** high oil prices made oil sands profitable "even though the amount of labour and capital needed to extract a barrel of oil was greater than in conventional production". — [Government of Alberta (2012), *Alberta Productivity Story 1997-2010*](https://open.alberta.ca/dataset/12c02d5e-12ce-4751-a4f4-3d2d12f09b16/resource/511df1f7-52b9-4f0a-94f4-cc498aee731e/download/6582918-2012-alberta-productivity-story-1997-2010-2012-11-14.pdf.)
- StatCan (Apr 2017): the sharp 2015 fall in business-sector MFP in the resource provinces "reflected a cyclical decline in their real GDP, due to a sharp decline in commodity prices". — [Statistics Canada, The Daily, 18 Apr 2017](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/daily-quotidien/170418/dq170418d-eng.htm)
- **Norway (OECD Economic Survey 2024):** "Because of low productivity growth, Norway's lead over the richer half of the OECD countries has shrunk from around **42% to 27%** over the past 15 years." Petroleum production and revenues are projected to fall. — [OECD ECOSCOPE blog, "Norway: rougher waters ahead", 20 Jun 2024](https://oecdecoscope.blog/2024/06/20/norway-rougher-waters-ahead/)
- **Norway Productivity Commission (NOU 2015:1):** productivity growth in market-oriented mainland industries fell from about **3%/yr (1996 to 2005)** to **0.8%** afterwards. "Our prosperity is primarily determined by productivity growth in the mainland economy." — [NOU 2015:1 summary](https://files.nettsteder.regjeringen.no/wpuploads01/blogs.dir/56/files/2015/04/summary_firstreport_productivityCommission.pdf)
- About **8% of mainland production** supplies the offshore sector (OECD Economic Survey of Norway 2014). — [OECD 2014](https://www.oecd-ilibrary.org/economics/oecd-economic-surveys-norway-2014/assessment-and-recommendations_eco_surveys-nor-2014-3-en)

### Inferences
- Canada's oil sands and Australia's mining boom show the same pattern. Price-induced extensive-margin expansion into lower-grade or higher-cost deposits lowers measured MFP while raising incomes. That is why terms-of-trade-adjusted income measures, and resource-rent-adjusted MFP, matter for cross-country comparisons.
- Norway separates "mainland" from petroleum GDP in its official statistics. That suggests a presentational option for Australia: report market-sector MFP excluding mining and utilities.

### Gaps
- I found no recent (2023 to 2026) StatCan or Bank of Canada estimates of resource-sector MFP, and no Dutch-disease studies (for example Beine, Bos & Coulombe 2012 on Canada). This was not searched further because of the budget.
- I did not retrieve resource-rent-adjusted productivity studies (for example Brandt, Schreyer & Zipperer 2017, OECD, "Productivity measurement with natural capital", *Review of Income and Wealth*). This is a strong lead to verify.
- OECD Canada survey (2025) productivity chapter not retrieved.

## 6. Resource-dependent Southeast Asia: Brunei, Malaysia, Indonesia downstreaming; Thailand's middle-income trap

### Takeaway
Brunei's IMF surveillance (latest found: 2024 Article IV) frames diversification away from oil and gas as "complex", against declining hydrocarbons and decarbonisation. Indonesia's nickel downstreaming has raised domestic value added through export bans, but the IMF has criticised it and World Bank work flags hidden productivity costs. The World Bank (Oct 2025) says Thailand's growth model has stalled, with productivity growth roughly halving.

### Cited Findings
- **Brunei, IMF 2024 Article IV (Country Report No. 24/301, Sept 2024):** diversification challenges are "complex". The IMF recommends a prudent fiscal stance given uncertainty around oil and gas revenues and long-term decarbonisation. Growth was driven mainly by non-oil and gas activity and an early new gas field. — [IMF Country Report 24/301](https://www.imf.org/en/Publications/CR/Issues/2024/09/24/Brunei-Darussalam-2024-Article-IV-Consultation-Press-Release-and-Staff-Report-555551); [Press release PR24/340](https://www.imf.org/en/news/articles/2024/09/25/pr-24340-brunei-imf-concludes-2024-article-iv-consultation/index.md)
  - Older IMF-sourced figure, from around 2010 and dated: oil and gas made up about 90% of government revenue and about 95% of export revenue. — [Oxford Business Group](https://drupal.oxfordbusinessgroup.com/node/876718)
- **Indonesia, IMF 2023 Article IV (June 2023):** the IMF criticised the nickel export ban for lacking cost-benefit analysis and for its trade spillovers, and recommended phasing out export restrictions and not extending them to other commodities. — [Jakarta Post analysis](https://www.thejakartapost.com/opinion/2023/07/12/analysis-ri-adamant-on-upholding-downstream-policy-despite-imf-criticism)
- **World Bank East Asia & Pacific Economic Update (April 2026):** downstreaming "could substantially enhance economic value creation". It recommends replacing distortive export restrictions with a policy mix encouraging greener, higher value-added mining. — [Indonesia Business Post](https://indonesiabusinesspost.com/6460/markets-and-finance/world-bank-suggests-policy-flexibility-despite-indonesia-s-downstreaming-gains)
- **World Bank / Zhejiang University presentation (Nov 2025):** this is a work in progress, not a finding. It hypothesises hidden costs: a lower post-ban steel price lowers the cutoff productivity in steel-using industries, so aggregate productivity falls (proxied by average firm size). — [World Bank slides](https://thedocs.worldbank.org/en/doc/6c4802573b0952b66e1106612422f12a-0050012025/related/4A-SlidesIndonesiaExportBanNickel.pdf)
- East Asia Forum says Indonesia's success relies on using its nickel "market power", and that smelting is emissions-intensive because of captive coal power. — [East Asia Forum (2023)](https://eastasiaforum.org/2023/09/28/assessing-nickel-downstreaming-in-indonesia/)
- **Thailand, World Bank (Oct 2025):** the growth model has stalled. Reaching high-income status by 2037 needs about 5% annual growth. The report cites stagnating productivity and over-reliance on low-cost manufacturing, and calls for opening services and lowering competition barriers. — [Nation Thailand](https://www.nationthailand.com/business/economy/40056544); [The Star](https://www.thestar.com.my/aseanplus/aseanplus-news/2025/10/09/world-bank-urges-thailand-to-chart-new-course-to-escape-middle-income-trap)
- **Thailand, OECD Economic Survey 2025:** productivity growth averaged **2.1% (2015 to 2023)** against **4.8% (2010 to 2015)**. — [OECD Economic Surveys: Thailand 2025](https://issuu.com/oecd.publishing/docs/oecd_economic_surveys_thailand_2025)

### Inferences
- Indonesia's downstreaming is a policy-induced version of the "resource-to-processing" step that Australia has mostly not taken. Its productivity effect is ambiguous: value added rises, but downstream allocative efficiency may fall. This bears on Australian debates about a Future Made in Australia and green metals.

### Gaps
- **Malaysia:** I found no source in this session on resource dependence, productivity, or high-income transition. Leads: the World Bank Malaysia Economic Monitor and the IMF Malaysia Article IV 2025.
- **Brunei 2025/2026 Article IV:** none found. The 2024 report is the latest located.
- No firm-level productivity evidence for Indonesian smelters was found.
- I did not retrieve the World Bank's 2024 *World Development Report: The Middle-Income Trap*. It is the canonical citation and should be added.
