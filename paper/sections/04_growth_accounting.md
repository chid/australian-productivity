# Neither engine is running: growth accounting {#sec:ga}

Table 2 reports OECD growth-accounting contributions for three windows. Figure 4 shows the 2020–24 window. We report 2022–24 separately because 2020–21 contain large COVID-era swings. The most extreme is Canada: in 2021 its MFP contribution was −13.0 percentage points and its capital-deepening contribution +8.9, which reflects how capital is measured when hours fall abruptly rather than any real change in the economy. Statistics Canada reports that capital intensity *lowered* Canadian labour productivity growth in each year from 2021 to 2023 [@NSFinance2025], consistent with our 2022–24 figures.

Three conclusions follow.

**Australia's MFP has been weak for twenty years, not five.** On ABS data, market-sector MFP averaged 1.0% a year over 1998–99 to 2003–04, −0.2% over 2003–04 to 2009–10, and 0.7% over 2009–10 to 2017–18 [@ABS2026mfp]. The PC reports a 20-year average of 0.4% a year, against 1.6% over 1994–95 to 2003–04 [@PC2026bulletin]. MFP fell in nine of sixteen industries in 2024–25. Composition adjustment makes the post-2019 picture worse, not better. The RBA's real-time labour-quality index attributes about two-thirds of market-sector labour productivity growth over 2018–19 to 2023–24 to rising education and experience, so quality-adjusted growth was about 0.3% a year [@BrunoHamburWang2025].

**Capital deepening has stalled.** The PC attributes the 1.1% rise in market-sector labour productivity in 2023–24 "almost entirely" to capital deepening [@PC2025bulletin]. Over longer windows, capital per worker has been "broadly unchanged" since well before the pandemic [@Plumb2025]. Christensen reports that market-sector capital productivity fell 18.6% between 1995 and 2025, and that market-sector R&D as a share of gross fixed capital formation fell by more than 40% [@Christensen2026]. On OECD 2020–24 averages, about two-thirds of the Australia–US gap is MFP and one-third capital deepening.

**The United States is the outlier.** BLS private-business TFP grew 1.0% a year over 2019–25, against 0.6% over 2007–19, and capital intensity grew 2.3–2.7% a year in 2024–25 [@BLS2026tfp]. The OECD's larger US MFP figure (+1.34 points) differs because it covers the whole economy, uses a different window, and does not separate out labour composition. The other comparators look like Australia. Canadian business MFP averaged about −0.1% a year over 2019–22 [@StatCan2024mfp]. UK market-sector MFP was 0.7% below its 2019 level in 2024 [@ONS2025mfp].

## Is it mismeasurement?

Leading tests of the mismeasurement hypothesis reject it as an explanation of the US slowdown. Correcting IT hardware prices makes that slowdown *larger*, and across countries the size of the slowdown is unrelated to ICT intensity [@ByrneFernaldReinsdorf2016; @Syverson2017]. The Australian evidence is consistent with this. Information media and telecommunications (+4.1%) and finance (+3.1%) were among the *strongest* MFP performers in 2024–25 [@ABS2026mfp]. Australia's material measurement problems lie elsewhere:

- **Non-market output.** The PC's quality-adjusted measure of healthcare productivity grows about 3% a year [@PC2024health].
- **Labour quality.** The ABS's labour-quality index interpolates between Censuses.
- **Mining and electricity output.** Measured output omits depletion, reliability and emissions (section [6](#sec:sectors)).
- **Hours and PPPs.** Revisions to hours worked and changes of PPP vintage move the figures.

Each of these corrections would raise measured growth in exactly the sectors where policy has expanded activity.
