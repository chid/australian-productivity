# International comparisons {#sec:compare}

: Table 3. Comparator economies: drivers of productivity growth and lessons for Australia

| Economy | Post-2019 pattern | Main drivers | Lesson for Australia |
|---|---|---|---|
| **United States** | Accelerating TFP and capital deepening | Business-formation surge; reallocation through workers quitting rather than being retained; strong investment in capital and intangibles | Reallocation and firm entry matter; JobKeeper-style job retention has costs |
| **Canada** | Weak MFP and negative capital deepening | Investment per worker collapse; population surge; interprovincial barriers | Rapid labour-force growth without investment produces capital shallowing |
| **Germany** | Falling MFP; industrial downturn | Energy-price shock on an energy-importing industrial base; labour hoarding | Energy costs and industry mix matter, but the mechanism differs from Australia's |
| **France / Italy** | Rising hours per head, falling MFP | Subsidised apprenticeships (France); low-productivity jobs | Policy-driven employment growth lowers measured productivity |
| **UK** | Stagnant MFP since 2008 | Reallocation, investment, energy | A long slowdown is not specific to Australia |
| **Singapore** | About 2% growth at the frontier | Targeted productivity and compute subsidies; skills | Direct support for diffusion, though a city-state is a weak comparator |
| **Vietnam / Indonesia** | Rapid catch-up | Foreign investment and manufacturing; supply chains diversifying away from China | Opportunities for engagement in the region |
| **Thailand / Brunei** | Stalled | Closed services sector; hydrocarbon dependence | A cautionary case for resource-heavy economies |

The comparison changes what needs explaining. Weak MFP and stalled capital deepening are common across the advanced economies; the United States is the exception. The forces behind the US acceleration were largely absent from *every* comparator, not only Australia: deep capital and intangible investment, reallocation into young firms, and early diffusion of digital technology [@BLS2026tfp; @DeckerHaltiwanger2023]. Australia's distinctive features are the additional drags set out in sections [5](#sec:hours)–[7](#sec:structural).

In Southeast Asia, Singapore sets a 2–3% annual productivity target and subsidises firms' access to AI and computing [@DISG2025eci; @SGesr2026]. The World Bank reports that Thailand's structural transformation has stalled [@WorldBank2025thailand]. Brunei shows how quickly hydrocarbon revenue can erode [@IMF2024brunei]. Indonesia's export bans to force downstream nickel processing raised domestic value added but drew IMF criticism for lacking cost–benefit analysis [@WorldBank2025nickel]. That is relevant to Australia's own green-metals ambitions.
