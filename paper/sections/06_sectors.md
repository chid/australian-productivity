# Mining, electricity and construction {#sec:sectors}

Cumulative MFP growth by industry over 2001–02 to 2023–24 ranges from +62% in information technology to −35% in utilities (Figure 6). The three industries with the largest declines fell for different reasons.

## Mining: depletion and investment lags, then weather and lower ore grades

Mining MFP fell 3.3% in 2024–25, its fifth consecutive decline, which the ABS attributes to weather disruptions [@ABS2026mfp]. Cumulatively it fell 34% between 2001–02 and 2023–24. Topp et al. showed that the 24% fall over 2000–01 to 2006–07 reflected declining ore yields and the lag between investment and production. Once those effects are removed, other factors *raised* mining MFP by about 8% [@Topp2008mining]. The ABS's experimental series that counts mineral and energy resources as capital substantially reduces the measured decline [@ABS2014mining]. Canada's oil sands show the same mechanism: when high prices make costly reserves profitable to extract, measured productivity falls [@CSLS2009]. Norway reports productivity for its "mainland" economy separately from petroleum, a convention Australia could follow by publishing MFP excluding mining and utilities [@NOU2015].

## Electricity: an investment phase that MFP records as decline

In the PC's experimental estimate, the electricity capital stock rose 126% while output rose 14%. Each dollar of output now requires about \$10 of capital, against just over \$2 for the economy as a whole. About 37% of investment since 2017–18 has gone to renewables and storage, whose capacity factors are lower than those of coal generation [@PC2026qbjun]. Adjusting for reliability using Australian Energy Regulator (AER) benchmarking reduces the decline in distribution productivity from 24% to 8%, and in transmission from 26% to 15%. Valuing the 30% fall in emissions at \$67 a tonne turns a 32% fall in electricity productivity into a 23% fall. The PC compares this period to "the 'investment phase' of a mining boom". Germany shows the same symptom, falling measured productivity, from a different cause. The IMF attributes about 60% of Germany's downturn to lower potential growth, with the 2022 energy-price shock amplifying it [@Kemp2026; @IMF2023germany]. Germany suffered a *price* shock as an energy importer. Australia is going through a *capital-intensive* transition as an energy exporter.

## Construction: dwellings per hour have halved

Dwellings completed per hour worked fell 53% over about thirty years, while economy-wide labour productivity rose 49% [@PC2025housing]. Productivity fell in detached-house building and rose in higher-density building, which points to regulation and site constraints rather than technology. Some critics argue that physical measures understate quality improvements [@Murray]. Because generation and transmission construction is classified as construction output, the energy transition competes with housing for the same workers [@PC2026qbjun].
