# Hours, migration and the care economy {#sec:hours}

## A surge in employment, not longer hours

Between March 2020 and March 2025, Australian hours worked rose 12.8% and employment 13.2%, so hours per worker were roughly flat [@ABS2025labour]. The PC describes "a record surge in hours worked" between September 2021 and June 2023. It attributes the surge to pent-up demand, record labour-force participation and migrant inflows, and judges migrant supply "broadly as expected" [@PC2025covid]. Canada's population grew 3.2% in 2023, but employment did not keep pace, so its hours per head fell [@McCormackWang2024]. In the euro area, employment rose while average hours per worker fell, so output per worker declined while output per hour *rose* [@ECBblog2024]. International comparisons must therefore be made per hour, not per worker.

## COVID composition effects washed out

In the PC's shift-share analysis, about 86% of the change in productivity between December 2019 and December 2020 came from employment moving between industries. Only 9% of the 6.1% fall between March 2022 and June 2023 did [@PC2025covid]. BLS, Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco and ONS studies document the same mechanism [@Stewart2022bls; @FRBSF2024; @ONS2021overview]. For a comparison of 2019 with 2025, these effects broadly net out. The PC and the RBA disagree about the 2022–23 fall. The PC emphasises inexperienced new entrants. The RBA's direct labour-quality measure attributes only about 0.4 points of a 3.5% fall to lower labour quality [@BrunoHamburWang2025]. We give more weight to the more direct measure.

## Migration: temporary capital dilution, better-evidenced misallocation

Official analyses confirm a temporary *capital-shallowing* effect: capital growth has not kept pace with the growth in hours worked. In Australia this reflects "long term investment weakness … rather than a COVID-19-specific phenomenon" [@PC2025covid]. The Bank of Canada modelled negative capital deepening in 2024 as population growth outpaced capital accumulation [@BoC2024san]. In both countries, investment per worker was weak before the migration surge. Claims that migration is the primary cause of the stagnation come from advocacy sources and are not supported by official analysis [@MacroBusiness2025].

The better-evidenced channel is *visa composition*. Matched employer–employee data show skilled-visa holders concentrated in more productive firms, and student and working-holiday visa holders in less productive firms [@e61misallocated2023]. This is misallocation, not dilution, and should be modelled separately. Treasury's Intergenerational Report migration scenarios explicitly exclude productivity effects [@Treasury2021igr].

## The care economy

The non-market sector accounted for about 55% of filled-jobs growth and 52.6% of hours growth between March 2020 and March 2025. Health care and social assistance supplied about 70% of that, with hours up 39% [@ABS2025labour]. The RBA estimates that the rising non-market share lowered economy-wide labour productivity growth by about 0.3 percentage points a year over 2017–18 to 2023–24, double the drag of the previous decade [@Plumb2025]. The PC traces the expansion to the National Disability Insurance Scheme (NDIS), new aged-care standards and childcare subsidies. It warns that if quality is mismeasured, "the post-pandemic decline in measured productivity is exaggerated" [@PC2025covid].

France offers a close European parallel. The Banque de France attributes 1.2 points of France's 8.5% productivity shortfall to subsidised apprenticeships and 1.7 points to labour hoarding [@DevulderEtAl2024]. Both countries expanded employment by policy in activities with low *measured* productivity. Unlike the euro area [@VoxEUhoarding], however, Australia's labour market shows strong demand rather than hoarding.
