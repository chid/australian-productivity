# Introduction {#sec:intro}

For three decades, output per hour worked was the main source of Australian prosperity. Since 2019 it has done almost no work. On OECD whole-economy data, Australian GDP per hour grew 0.10% a year between 2019 and 2025. Over the same years, US output per hour rose from US\$76.6 to US\$85.6 (constant 2020 PPP), an average of 1.88% a year [@OECDpdb]. The Reserve Bank of Australia (RBA) now describes labour productivity as "currently sitting at around 2019 levels". In August 2025 it cut its trend productivity assumption from 1.0% to 0.7% [@RBA2026senate]. The most recent data have not turned. Labour productivity fell 0.2% over the year to June 2026, because hours worked grew faster than output [@PC2026update].

This paper makes three contributions. First, it reconciles the headline international statistics with the domestic statistics most often quoted in Australian debate. The two give materially different numbers: about 0.1% a year for the whole economy on OECD calendar-year data, and about 0.2–0.5% a year for the ABS market sector, depending on the start year [@ABS2026mfp]. We show that the difference comes from six identifiable sources and that every one of them pushes the OECD measure down. We also show that the gap between Australia and the United States is robust across sources, even though Australia's own growth rate is not.

Second, the paper places Australia in a broader comparison than is usual. Most Australian commentary benchmarks against the United States. We add Canada, a resource exporter whose population growth is also driven by immigration; Europe, where the energy shock hit and labour hoarding and subsidised employment are documented; and Southeast Asia, where Singapore operates at the productivity frontier while resource-dependent economies have stalled. That wider comparison changes the diagnosis. Australia failed *alongside* most of the advanced world, while the United States broke away.

Third, the paper separates the parts of the stagnation that are common to many countries from those specific to Australia, and real stagnation from artefacts of statistical convention. Four Australian features stand out. The first is a care economy that grew by policy design and whose output is measured by its inputs. The second is mining and electricity, both in capital-heavy phases. The third is a non-mining investment share that never recovered from the global financial crisis. The fourth is two decades of weakening competition, firm entry and diffusion of technology to firms behind the frontier.
