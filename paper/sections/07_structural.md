# Investment, competition, dynamism and diffusion {#sec:structural}

## Investment and intangibles

Non-mining business investment has fallen 3.2 percentage points as a share of GDP since the GFC, and capital deepening, historically Australia's main source of productivity growth, "has stalled" [@RobsonSterland2025]. The gap with the United States is probably widest in intangible investment, such as software, R&D and organisational capital. US intangible investment reached 15.6% of GDP in 2025, more than tangible investment [@WIPO2026]. The latest Australian estimate is for 2005–06 [@BarnesMcClure2009], which is itself a gap in the evidence. Canada is the cautionary comparator. Its business investment per worker fell from 87% to 54% of the US level between 2014 and 2024 [@Fraser2026investment, advocacy-leaning; see also @RobsonBafale2025].

## Competition and markups

Australian firm-level data (from the ABS business-longitudinal dataset, BLADE) show the top-four firm share of industry sales rising about 2 points, to 43%, between 2001–02 and 2018–19. Average markups rose about 5–6%, and more than twice as fast in digitally intensive sectors [@DayEtAl2022; @Hambur2023er]. Weaker reallocation of activity between firms reduced annual labour productivity growth by about 0.1 points, roughly a fifth of the slowdown since 2012. Calibrating an Edmond–Midrigan–Xu model, Hambur and Freestone estimate that productivity and GDP would be 1–3% higher had competition stayed at mid-2000s levels [@HamburFreestone2025]. King [@King2023] argues that the increases in concentration "are not huge". We read competition as a material but not dominant explanation.

## Diffusion to laggards

Across 23 countries, global frontier firms kept accelerating in the 2000s while laggards stalled [@AndrewsCriscuoloGal2016]. Australia fits this pattern closely. Between 2002 and 2016, labour productivity rose 60% at the global frontier, 25% at Australian frontier firms and 15% at Australian laggards. The speed at which laggards caught up fell about 9%, and declining dynamism and competition explain one-half to three-quarters of that slowdown [@AndrewsHamburHansellWheeler2022]. The firm entry rate fell from 14.5% to 11.5% over the same period, and job switching fell from 11.3% to 8.5% by 2019 [@e61mobility2025]. In the United States, by contrast, business formation surged after 2020, shifting activity toward young firms [@DeckerHaltiwanger2023]. The PC argues that Australia's JobKeeper wage subsidy limited the equivalent reallocation [@PC2025covid].

## AI and technology adoption

Australian business use of AI rose from 1% in 2022–23 to 12% in 2024–25 [@ABS2026cab]. In the United States, faster productivity growth is concentrated in industries with high AI adoption, but AI explains little of the aggregate acceleration so far [@CakirMelekMiller2026]. Micro-level gains are large: generative AI raised customer-support productivity by 14%, and by 34% for novices [@BrynjolfssonLiRaymond]. Macro estimates are modest, with a TFP gain of at most about 0.7% over ten years [@Acemoglu2025]. The productivity J-curve [@BrynjolfssonRockSyverson2021] predicts that measured TFP falls while firms build the complementary intangible capital that new technology requires. Australia's weak intangible investment is therefore likely to be the binding constraint on gains from AI.
